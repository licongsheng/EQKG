**1.** **Introduction**


A B S T R A C T

Exposure to extremely low frequency magnetic fields (ELF-MF) may have different effects on spermatozoa
depending on the waveform, magnetic flux density, frequency of ELF-MF, and duration of exposure. In this study,
we investigated the possible role of ELF-MF (50 Hz; 1 mT) exposure in altering sperm parameters. In this study
we found that exposure to ELF-MF at the frequency of 50 Hz (1 mT) for two hours induces statistically significant
alterations in progressive motility, morphology and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production of human spermatozoa, suggesting a role of ELF-MF in altering reproductive function of spermatozoa. Our results represent an
important discovery in the field since occupational exposure to the sine waveform 1 mT 50 Hz ELF-MF used in
our study is possible in workplace. Moreover, these electromagnetic fields are product by many electronic devices and household appliances. Thus, alterations of progressive motility and morphology of spermatozoa would
be important consequences of human exposures to ELF-MF.


In the last years, many researchers investigated the effects on human
health of the exposure to electromagnetic fields emitted by electronic
devices and household appliances, known as extremely low frequency
Golbach
magnetic fields (ELF-MF), on cells and molecular mechanisms (
et al., 2015; Goodman et al., 2009; Nakayama et al., 2016). Several
studies investigated the implication of ELF-MF in both animals and
human with contrasting results. Many researches have reported that
chronic exposure to electromagnetic fields has caused negative effects
on reproduction and alteration of reproductive parameters. In fact, de
Bruyn at al. showed that exposure to ELF-MF could deteriorated sperm
parameters in mice models, including vitality, which decreased, and
quality of sperm motility (de Bruyn and de Jager, 2010). Instead,
another study highlighted that there are no adverse effects on spermatogenesis after ELF-MF exposure in mice (Duan et al., 2014). In
addition, several studies investigated the involvement of ELF-MF in
different human diseases (e.g., neurodegenerative disease and angiocardiopathies) obtaining conflicting results (Bagheri Hosseinabadi et al.,
2019; Giorgi et al., 2014). Moreover, ELF-MF exposure in the


workplaces can be higher than the general population (Modenese and
Gobba, 2021; Stam, 2014). However, the effects of ELF-MF exposure on
human reproduction, specifically on men, are still matter of debate.

In human studies, a significant increase of human sperm motility was
observed following exposure (for 3 h) to ELF-MF of 5 mT magnetic flux
density and 50 Hz of frequency (Iorio et al., 2007) another study found
no association between ELF-MF and human sperm motility (2.5 mT, 50
Hz for 3 h) (Iorio et al., 2011). Li et al. showed an inverse correlation
between ELF-MF exposure and sperm motility and morphology (Li et al.,
2010). On top of that, a recent study by Gorski et al. found a negative ´
effect of ELF-MF (50 Hz for 5, 15 and 30 min) on human sperm motility
(Gorski et al., 2020). The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of
50 Hz (1 mT) ELF-MF on human spermatozoa.

main biases related to human sperm exposure to electromagnetic fields
(Pacchierotti et al., 2021). Semen samples were obtained by masturbation after 3-day abstinence and were collected in sterile containers.
Sperm concentration and motility were evaluated using Makler counting
chamber (SEFI Medical Instruments, Tel Aviv, Israel) and
computer-assisted semen analysis (CASA) (Delli Muti et al., 2014a,b).

Semen samples (n = 30) were obtained from healthy donors (which
semen parameters were all above the 5th percentile) and were divided
into two aliquots of 0.5 ml each, inside 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes, sealed
with parafilm. Therefore, the first aliquot of seminal fluid was exposed
to a 1.0 mT 50 Hz ELF-MF for two hours at 37.0 [◦] C, using a self-designed
square-shaped Helmholtz coil (Fig. 1) suitable for in vitro tests on
human cells inside an incubator (Elexpuru-Zabaleta et al., 2022). Specifically, the inner dimensions of the Helmholtz coil and the distances
between the two solenoids (34.0 × 34.0 cm and 19.5 cm, respectively)
were chosen from data reported in the literature (Abbott, 2015). The
optimal diameter of the copper wire was calculated to keep the dissipation of the electric power within 8 Watts, in this way the maintenance
of a constant temperature of 37.0 ± 0.1 [◦] C of the spermatozoa was

–
guaranteed. Few samples (2 4 each time) were exposed and placed in
the central point between the two solenoids, ensuring a homogenous
ELF-MF between samples. The Helmholtz coil was powered by a variable


transformer (variac) connected to the mains (230 Volts AC, 50 Hz) and
set to generate 50 Hz sine ELF-MF at a magnetic flux density of 1.0 mT.
The ELF-MF was verified with a professional ELF-MF analyzer (EFA-300;
Wandel & Goltermann, Germany). The second aliquot of the sample
(control sample) was stored for two hours at 37.0 [◦] C in an incubator
without ELF-MF (the 50 Hz ELF-MF generated by incubator was 0.792
µT). The maintenance of a constant temperature of 37.0 ± 0.1 [◦] C for
both groups of samples (exposed and controls) was verified by using
Thermochron iButton DS1922L (Maxim Integrated, San Jose, CA, USA).

At the end of the ELF-MF treatment, exposed and control samples
were coded and transferred to an operator that, in a blinded condition,
routinely processed them using light microscope to determine sperm
quality of each aliquot. According to the World Health Organization
(WHO) criteria (Organization, 2021), normal sperm parameters were
defined as sperm cell concentration ≥ 16 × 10 [6 ] cells/ml; and sperm
cells with typical morphology *>* 4 %; ‘a+b [′] type motility or progressive
motility ≥ 30 %; ‘c [′] type motility or non-progressive sperm motility.
Semen cultures were negative for the presence of *Chlamydia* and *Ure-*
*aplasma urealyticum,* as well as the mixed antiglobulin reaction (MAR)

test.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in spermatozoa were investigated using MitoSOX red, a fluorescent dye able to detect mitochondrial
superoxide/ROS formation. Briefly, sperm samples (n = 8) were counted and divided into two aliquots (ELF-MF exposed and control) containing 1 × 10 [6 ] cells. After two hours, ELF-MF exposed and control
samples were washed three times with HBSS (Merck KGaA Darmstadt,
Germany), plated in 96-well black plate (Corning, Glendale, Arizona,
USA) and stained with 2.5 µM of MitoSOX red (Thermo Fisher Scientific,
Milan, Italy), at 37 [◦] C for 20 min. Fluorescence at excitation/emission
wavelengths of 535 ± 25/590 ± 20 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Sunrise; Tecan Group Ltd). Three replicates were performed for each sample.

The mean values obtained for the exposed and control samples were
compared using the Wilcoxon non-parametric test for paired data. Data
was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and tests were
considered as statistically significant with P *<* 0.05. Statistical analysis
was carried out using the statistical package SPSS for Windows (version
14.0).

**3.** **Results**

Following exposure of the samples, the averages of the sperm parameters studied were compared (Fig. 2). At first, no statistical differences were found for the mean of sperm concentration (Fig. 2a) while a
statistically significant decrease was observed in the mean percentage of
spermatozoa with typical morphology (13.3 % vs 15.6 %) (Fig. 2b).
Interestingly, we found a statistically significant difference in total
sperm motility (Fig. 2c) and in the average percentage of progressive
motility of the exposed samples (26.2 %) compared to the unexposed
ones (38.4 %) (Fig. 2d). We also found a statistically significant decrease
in the average percentage of non-progressive motility of the exposed
samples (10.9 %) compared to the non-exposed ones (12.6 %) (Fig. 2d).

Finally, we investigated the effects of ELF-MF in inducing oxidative
stress. Interestingly, we found that ELF-MF exposure significantly
increased the ROS production (18.0 %) in the exposed samples
compared to non-exposed ones (Fig. 2e).

**4.** **Discussion**


Emerging data highlighted that exposure to ELF-MF may have a
negative or positive effect on spermatozoa depending on the waveform,
frequency of ElF-MF, and duration of exposure (Darbandi et al., 2018).
Our data indicate that exposure to ELF-MF waves at the frequency of
50 Hz (1mT) for two hours induces statistically significant alterations in
spermatozoa progressive motility and morphology. The cause of these

the inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) induction (Yoshikawa et al.,
2000). NO is an important modulator of numerous biological processes
including vasodilation, inhibition of platelet activation and aggregation,
inhibition of adhesion molecule expression, inhibition of smooth muscle
cell proliferation, reduced oxidation of LDL cholesterol and increased
insulin sensitivity (Forstermann and Sessa, 2012). Furthermore, an
important role of NO and oxidative stress in sperm motility, capacitation
and acrosome reaction has already been highlighted (Balercia et al.,
2004; Buldreghini et al., 2010). The high content of polyunsaturated
fatty acids in sperm give these cells more susceptible to oxidative stress
and lipid peroxidation due to ROS action (Alvarez and Storey, 1995).

In regards to sperm motility, researchers have found discordant results. In fact, some studies in animals had found a reduction in sperm
motility due to oxidative stress, instead in other studies no differences
were found (Bernabo et al., 2010; de Bruyn and de Jager, 2010; Roychoudhury et al., 2009; Torres et al., 2019). Lastly, studies showed a
linkage between ELF-MF and sperm concentration in mice and rats
(Al-Akhras et al., 2006; Hong et al., 2003), but other studies showed no
increase or decrease in sperm count (Akdag et al., 2006; Chung et al.,


2005; Torres et al., 2019). Our study shows an increase, although not
significant, in the sperm concentration. Thus, increased concentrations
of NO and ROS, due to the exposure to ELF-MF, may cause oxidative
stress inducing sperm DNA damage and apoptosis of spermatic germ
cells leading to a reduced sperm motility and alterations in morphology
and viability, resulting in a severe alterations of the reproductive function of spermatozoa (Patruno et al., 2015).

However, studies suggest that ELF-MF exposure can positively or
negatively modulate sperm parameters. In rats Gholami et al. found an
increase in total and progressive motility in sperm exposed to an ELF-MF
with square waveform, 5 mT intensity and 50 Hz frequency (Gholami
et al., 2019). Similar results on motility have been observed by Iorio
et al. in human semen exposed to an ELF-EMF with a square waveform of
5 mT and frequency of 50 Hz (Iorio et al., 2007). The influence of
ELF-MF on spermatozoa motility were related to an ELF-MF action on
mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (Iorio et al., 2011). In fact, in
animal models, Torres et al. showed a decrease in the mitochondrial
activity index of sperm exposed to ELF-MF with a decrease in sperm
motility (Torres et al., 2019). A reduction in sperm motility and in activity ratio of spermatozoa following an exposure to a 50 Hz ELF of 0.4
mT has been showed by Xu et al. (2012). Similar results were obtained

by Li et al. that showed a two-fold increased risk of abnormal sperm
motility and morphology in subjects with ELF-MF exposure ≥ 1.6 mG (Li
et al., 2010). Darbandi et al. reviewed studies on the effects of ELF-MF
on male fertilities published from 1978 to 2016 (Darbandi et al.,
2018). They concluded that about 50 Hz ELF-MF only that with a square
waveform had positive effects on sperm quality, motility, and fertility.
Other exposures to 50 or 60 Hz ELF-MF either had no effects or caused
adverse effects on sperm quality (Darbandi et al., 2018). The ELF-MF
effect on sperm parameters likely depends both on the characteristics
of ELF-MF (i.e., frequency and wave type) and on the doses and exposure
mode (i.e. flow density and timing). Since some people (e.g. welders)
can be exposed to the sine waveform 1mT 50 Hz ELF-MF used in our
study, and the alterations found in sperm morphology and progressive
motility would be important consequences of exposures to ELF-MF,
further investigations are needed.
