# Effect of whole-body 1800 MHz GSM-like microwave exposure on testicular steroidogenesis and histology in mice
## Zsolt Forg´acs [a] [,] [∗], Zolt´an Somosy [b], Gy¨orgyi Kubinyi [b], J´ozsef Bakos [b], Aranka Hud´ak [c], Andr´as Surj´an [a], Gy¨orgy Thur´oczy [b]

b *National “Frederic Joliot-Curie” Research Institute for Radiobiology and Radiohygiene, Budapest, Hungary*

Received 19 April 2005; received in revised form 30 November 2005; accepted 6 December 2005

Available online 24 January 2006

**Abstract**

The aim of our study was to evaluate the possible effects of whole-body 1800 MHz GSM-like microwave exposure on male reproduction. After
repeated exposure of mice to microwaves at 0.018–0.023 W/kg whole-body specific energy absorption rate (SAR) an elevated serum testosterone
level was measured, but no microwave exposure related histopathological alteration could be detected in the reproductive organs. The in vitro
steroidogenic response of 48 h Leydig cell cultures obtained from exposed animals did not differ from the controls, suggesting that Leydig cells
were not the primary targets of the applied microwave exposure or direct action of microwaves on Leydig cells was temporary only. In exposed
animals the red blood cell count and volume of packed red cells were also increased. Further investigations are required to clarify the mechanism
of action of the applied microwave exposure on male mice, as well as to establish the biological significance of the observed phenomena.

Exposure to high density microwaves can cause detrimental
effects on biological systems through thermal action [2,3]. With

quantified in terms of the amount of energy absorbed by a unit
mass of the object. This is expressed as the specific absorption
rate (SAR) with units of W/kg. The SAR value is independent
from the duration (or repetition) of exposure. In tissue, SAR is
proportional to the square of the internal electric field strength.
Values of SAR depend on the incident field parameters (frequency, intensity, polarization, source–object configuration), the
characteristics of the exposed body (size, internal and external
geometry, dielectric properties of tissues) and ground effects and
reflector effects of other objects in the field near the exposed
body. Average SAR and SAR distribution can be computed or

Below the safety levels thermal effect of microwaves on biological systems cannot be expected. However, there are many
reports over the last three decades that microwave (or radiofrequency) exposure can exert non-thermal influences, at intensities well below those necessary to cause any detectable heating. In previous investigations it was found that non-thermal
microwave (or high frequency) exposure is able to induce several changes at the level of DNA and protein molecules including
the increase DNA single and double strand breaks in rat brain

[6], induction of micronuclei in rat bone marrow [7], alteration
of protein conformation without bulk heating [8] or induction of
oxidative stress in rat brain tissue [9]. The molecular biological
changes can evoke a number of alterations at the cellular level,
such as increased Ca [2+] efflux in cultures of human neuroblastoma cells [10], reduced melatonin secretion [11] or disturbed
balance of chemicals in the dopamine-opiate system [12]. The
consequences of the cellular changes may be manifested as different functional alterations at the level of organs and organism,
like altered natural rhythms of the brain’s electrical activity [13],
increased permeability of the human blood–brain barrier [14,15]
or promotion of lymphomas in transgenic mice [16]. Compared
with continuous-wave (CW) radiation, pulsed microwave fields
with the same average rate of energy deposition in tissues are
generally more effective in producing a biological response,
especially when there is a well-defined threshold that must be
exceeded to elicit the effect [5]. Thus, it is quite possible that
living organisms have a two-fold sensitivity to the extremely low
frequency pulsed GSM signal [1].

GSM-services (internet, multimedia, GPS-combined GSM navigation systems, etc.) in addition to the telephone conversations
on workdays.

**2.** **Materials and methods**

*2.1.* *Animals*

Male NMRI mice (Charles-River, Hungary) weighing 35–41 g (9–10 week
old) were kept in a room with a 12-h light:12-h dark photoperiod (lights on
7:00–19:00), temperature of 20–22 [◦] C and relative humidity of 50–60% *.* The
NMRI strain of mouse is commonly used in radiation and bioelectromagnetic
research. In each experiment, 11–12 sham-exposed (control) and another 11–12
exposed mice were kept in plastic cages and allowed free access to standard
laboratory pellets (Altromin [®] ) and tap water. Five independent experiments
were performed. The mice were kept and handled according to the guidelines
of the Hungarian Law of Animal Care.

*2.2.* *1800 MHz GSM-like microwave exposure*

The animals were exposed 10 times (over 2 weeks on workdays) and the
duration of exposure was 2 h/day.

The field of mobile phones was simulated in a gigahertz transverse electromagnetic mode (GTEM) cell of 2 m height and 1.2 m × 1.2 m of floor space
(model S-GTEM; TKI-Network Ltd., Hungary), which is widely used for electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) tests [24]. The GTEM cell was positioned
vertically therefore the E field strength was horizontal within the exposure area.
The 1800 MHz radiation was produced by a mobile test phone (model NOKIA
7110;NokiaMobilePhonesLtd.)connectedtoaCommunicationTestSet(model
4202S; Wavetek, Germany) and the signal was amplified by a power amplifier
(model AS0204-17R; Milmega Ltd., UK).

The power was measured by a power meter (model HP 5247A; HewlettPackard, USA) and a radio-frequency (RF) spectrum analyzer (model U4941;
Advantest, Japan) Fig. 1. The RF carrier signal was pulsed at 217 Hz with bursts
of 576 �s pulsewidth and speech was not simulated (GSM-217 Hz signal).

During RF exposure 11–12 freely moving mice were kept in a pure (i.e.
lacking any metallic fittings) plastic cage of 40 cm × 30 cm × 10 cm, which were
placed over the absorbent material of the GTEM cell Fig. 2. The average power
density was 100 �W/cm [2] (19.4 V/m) within the cage. Within the GTEM cell, the
cage was positioned into that level where a given input power could generate the
maximum field strength over the whole area of cage. According to the dimension
of the GTEM cell and the position level of the cage, the required input power

*2.4.* *Quantification of testosterone*

Determination of testosterone directly from aliquots of the culture media and
blood sera was performed by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The
ELISA kits were purchased from Dialab (Austria). The samples were measured
at 450 nm by an Anthos 2010 microplate reader and the data were evaluated by
WinRead 2.3 computer software. The sensitivity of the T assay was 0.2 ng/ml
and the inter- and intra-assay coefficient of variations were 3.9 and 6.2%, respectively. Cross-reactivity with 5�-dihydroxytestosterone was 10%.

*2.5.* *Histological techniques*

Testes (left), epididymes, adrenals, prostates, kidneys, hearts, livers and pituitary glands were fixed in buffered formol saline for histological examination.
These tissues were dehydrated in ethanol, embedded in paraffin wax and stained
with hematoxylin and eosin.

*2.6.* *Hematology*

Blood was taken from aorta, collected in plastic Eppendorf tubes containing
K 3 EDTA. White (WBC) and red (RBC) blood cells counts, mean cell volume
(MCV), hemoglobine concentration (HGB), volume of packed red cells (VPRC)
and platelets (PLT) were determined using the Animal Blood Counter Vet abc [TM]

(Roche).

*2.7.* *Serum chemistry*

At sacrifice, free-flowing blood was collected in plastic Eppendorf tubes.
After centrifugation, serum was removed and the samples were then analyzed
for serum alkaline phosphatase, glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase (GOT),
glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (GPT), cholesterol and triglycerides by an auto
analyzer instrument (A.L. Instruments, France).

*2.8.* *Statistics*

All data were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using
GraphPad Prism 3.01 computer software. A value of *p* < 0.05 was considered to
be significant. Each experimental group has been passed over the normality test

**3.** **Results**

*3.1.* *Serum testosterone*

In the 1800 MHz GSM-like microwave exposed mice twoway ANOVA revealed significantly elevated ( *p* < 0.05) serum
testosterone level (7.85 ± 1.08 ng/ml) compared with the shamexposed controls (5.12 ± 0.79 ng/ml). Five independent experiments were performed, the total number of the samples was 56
(exposed) and 52 (sham-exposed).

*3.2.* *Testosterone production of interstitial cell culture*

The Leydig cells used in all experiments were viable (>90%
viability; checked by trypan blue exclusion test) and steroidogenically active. In the cell cultures obtained from animals subjected to whole-body microwave exposure, two-way ANOVA
demonstrated no significant change in the basal or in vitro
hCG stimulated T response (Fig. 3) and initial cell yield also
remained unaltered. No microwave exposure related alteration
was observed by phase contrast microscopy in cell size, attachment and density of the 48 h semiconfluent monolayers.

*3.3.* *Histology*

No microwave exposure related histopathological alterations
were found in testicles, epididymes, adrenals, prostates, kidneys,
hearts, livers and pituitary glands.

*3.4.* *Body weight*

There was no significant difference in the body weights of
exposed animals compared to the sham-exposed controls on the
first and the last day of the exposure.

**4.** **Discussion**

Our data obtained from five independent experiments show
that repeated exposure to 1800 MHz GSM-like microwave radiation at 0.018–0.023 W/kg whole-body SAR value is able to
elevate the serum testosterone level within the physiological
range in male mice. To study a possible direct effect of the
whole-body microwave exposure on testicular steroidogenesis,
the in vitro testosterone production was also determined in 48 h
Leydig cell cultures obtained from testicles of exposed mice. In
toxicological experiments, in vitro measures of T production of
Leydig cells seem to be more sensitive than in vivo indicators of
testicular damage [30]. Determination of the LH or hCG stimulated T response of cells improves the sensitivity of this method.
In present study, the unaltered in vitro steroidogenic capacity
of Leydig cells obtained from microwave exposed mice suggest
that Leydig cells were not the primary targets of the applied
microwave exposure or the direct action was transient only and
it was confined on the period of exposure. The mechanism of
action of the applied microwave exposure on serum testosterone
level cannot be discerned from present results. By contrast, in a
previous study we found an elevated hCG stimulated T response
of Leydig cell cultures obtained from whole-body 50 Hz magnetic field (MF) exposed mice, while the serum T level of the
animals remained unaltered. In that experiment, we suggested a
presumably direct effect of whole-body MF exposure on Leydig cells [31]. This hypothesis was supported by our previous
in vitro data [32]. Although, the GSM-like microwave radiation
applied in present study also contains a 217 Hz extremely low
frequency (ELF) component, the site of action seems to be basically different from the main target(s) of the 50 Hz MF exposure.

Considering present results and the previous data on the
effects of microwaves on central nervous system, the main targets of action are probably at the higher regulation level of the
hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. The effect of GSM-like
microwave exposure on serum testosterone level may be asso

ciated with its possible effect on melatonin secretion of pineal
gland. Some recent human data indicate that prolonged use of
GSM mobile phones may lead to reduced melatonin production

[11,33]. It is known that melatonin exerts an antigonadotrophic
effect mainly at the level of the hypothalamus and pituitary

[34–36] and decreased directly the testosterone secretion in Leydig cells too [37,38].

The non-thermal, pulsed microwave effects are connected
with the transfer of information from irradiating field to the alive
organism, through the latter’s ability to “recognise” certain frequency of radiation, than with its ability to absorb energy from
the field [39]. The intensity of radiation needed for this recognition is many orders of magnitude below even that currently
associated with non-thermal effect [1].

At cellular level, one of the most critical target of the MF or
microwave exposure in living systems the modification of cellular Ca [2+] fluxes. It is known that Ca [2+] -signalling has extreme sensitivity for coherent excitation by very low field energies within
specific amplitude and frequency windows [40]. The level of the
lowest published results for calcium ion efflux (in frog hearts) is
0.00015 W/kg (0.08 �W/cm [2] ) at 16 Hz modulated 240 MHz RF
signal [41]. The mechanism of action of gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) on the anterior pituitary gonadotropes has
been studied in great detail and it is generally accepted that Ca [2+]

functions as a second messenger in the GnRH-activated signal
transduction cascade [42–44].

The lack of any microwave related histopathological alterations in the investigated organs suggest that the applied GSMlike microwave exposure evoked only some functional changes
in the irradiated mice. It may be hypothesized that the observed
increase in serum testosterone levels was not due to an inciden
tal non-specific stress. Previous investigators found that stress
accompanied by a low serum testosterone level in men [45] and
in vitro hCG stimulated androgenesis of Leydig cells obtained
from stressed rats was also significantly reduced [46].

We recorded a slight, but statistically significant elevation
of RBC and VPRC in microwave exposed mice. Although
Friedman [47] in a small case-control study reported a possible microwave related polycythaemia among military radar
workers, in contrast to our findings several previous investigators did not find any changes in most of the blood variables
of non-thermal microwave irradiated animals [48–51]. The discrepancy between our data and the results of other reports may
be due to the different experimental conditions and/or the various species (or strains) of animals used. Non-thermal effects
have proved controversial, and independent attempts to replicate them have not always been successful. Such difficulties are
not unexpected, however, because these effects depend on the
state of the organism when it is exposed, particularly in vivo. The
highly non-linear nature of living systems makes them hypersensitive to the prevailing conditions, and thus militates against
the realisation of the identical conditions necessary for exact
replication [1].

To clarify the mechanism and site of action of the applied
GSM-like exposure on serum testosterone level additional
determination of other endocrine parameters (e.g. FSH, LH
and melatonin levels) is necessary. A following study with
