1. Introduction


A B S T R A C T

The dramatically increased use of electricity is raising major concerns as to the consequences of the interaction
between electromagnetic field (EMF) and neurobiology. The aim of this study is to investigate the effects of
magnetic field on working memory in the hippocampal region by analyzing local field potentials (LFPs) and
spikes pattern in vivo.

In present study, mice were exposed to EMF (50 Hz, 1 mT), static magnetic field (SMF, 1 mT), or placed in the
exposure tube but without EMF exposure (SHAM), respectively. During the exposure for 7 consecutive days,
mice were subjected to perform working memory (WM) tasks in Y-maze, and multichannel electrophysiology
signals from hippocampus of mice were recorded during the test, from which LFPs, spike firing rates, band power
at different frequencies, and theta-gamma modulation index (MI) were analyzed in details.

From our results, correct choice rate during WM task was found significantly decreased in EMF group after 3day exposure, which was consistent with noticeable decline in firing rate. Starting from Day 3 after EMF exposure, the power of theta (4–12 Hz) and gamma (LG, 30–60 Hz) before reference point (RP) in Y-maze were
also found to be descending, together with decrease of oscillatory activities of theta and gamma frequencies.

The results indicated that MI between theta and gamma could play a significant role in modulating the spikes
discharge and encoding WM. Therefore, the analysis of theta-gamma coupling and its oscillation strength may
provide a new perspective for mechanistic investigation of EMF-induced WM deficits.

2. Materials and methods

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guideline
under Institution Animal Care and Use Committee-approved protocols
at Nanjing University of Aeronautics and Astronautics.

2.1. Subjects

Adult male Institute of cancer research (ICR) mice (19–21 g) were
housed in cages with food and water ad libitum and maintained in a
room with natural light cycle and constant temperature (24 [°] C). All mice
were allowed a 3-day adaptation before experiments.

Prior to behavior testing, LFPs and spikes recording, the mice were
anesthetized with chloral hydrate (400 mg/kg) under aseptic condition,
mounted in the stereotactic frame, and gently implanted with 2 × 4
nickel-cadmium microelectrode array (impedance < 1 MΩ) and the
tip-to-tip distance between electrodes in the array is 200 μm. The microelectrode array was placed in the hippocampal region of the mice at
the following coordinates: anterioposterior: − 1 to − 2 mm from
bregma, lateral: 0.8–1.2 mm, horizontal: 1.5–2 mm, and then fixed to
the skull with dental acrylic. The mice were habituated to the microelectrode apparatus over the next 3 days.

According to individual difference in memory and athletic ability in
case of the surgery injury existence, the Y maze training session (refer
to section “Behavioral Detection”) was implemented to measure the
average correct rate of each mice before grouping. Then outlier mice
were eliminated.

2.2. Electromagnetic field exposure system

The electromagnetic field was created by an in-house built system
consisting of a sinusoidal waveform signal generator and a coil electromagnet [14]. As shown in Fig. 1B, the solenoid coil was made from a
1200 turned copper wire (1.01 mm) wrapped around a circular magnetic core. The internal dimension inside the coil
electromagnet allowing for following animal exposure is a fixing
bracket of a pipe for preventing mice from free moving. The length of
pipe is 8 cm and the diameter is 6 cm. The coil electromagnet was
supplied with a sinusoidal waveform signal generator so that the frequency and intensity of electromagnetic field can be modulated

[36,37]. By varying the input current, the magnetic flux density (MFD)


could be set anywhere from the ambient level to the maximum coildesigned magnetic field strength of 10 mT at the center of the coil. The
MFD in present study was monitored by a Gauss Meter (GM05, Tregoniggie Industrial Estate, Falmouth Cornwall, United Kingdom). The

–
local geo-MFD was approximately 0.04 0.05 mT (Seismological Bureau
of Yunnan Province, Yunnan, PR China).

2.3. Static magnetic field exposure system

A permanent ferrite magnet of cylindrical shape (diameter: 5 cm,
height: 3.5 cm) was used to create a static magnetic field (SMF), providing a unidirectional field with intensity between 0.1 and 50.6 mT
over a distance of 5 cm. The MFD to the pole surface was measured with
the same Gauss Meter as described above. To achieve a SMF with MFD

of 1 mT, the center of the N pole surface of the magnet was positioned
at a distance of 10 cm from the exposure area of mice head [38].

2.4. Behavioral testing

For animal behavioral detection, 21 mice were firstly divided into
following three groups, i.e. EMF group, SMF group, and SHAM group.
In the EMF group, mice were exposed to an ELF-EMF with an intensity
of 1 mT and a frequency of 50 Hz for 2 h/day. For the mice in the SMF
group, the same intensity of MFD (1 mT) and exposure time (2 h/day)
were applied. In the SHAM group, the mice were placed under same
experimental apparatus as to the one in EMF group but without any
power supply. The exposure process continued for 7 concussive days for
all groups.

Y-maze test was used to study how the mice function with WM
under various magnetic field exposure conditions. As shown in Fig. 1D,
the Y-maze consisted of three arms (stem length: 60 cm, branch length:
20 cm long). Each arm was a corridor made of polymethyl methacrylate.

Mice were maintained on a restricted diet and kept at 85% of freefeeding body weight during behavioral testing to keep the appetitive
motivation. Prior to testing, mice were given a 30 min training session
in order to achieve reliable data after microelectrode array implantation [19,35]. The training session was performed for 10 times per day
over 3 days. Each training session consisted of a “sample run” and a
“choice run” trial. In the “sample run” trial, the mice were allowed to
explore the Y-maze apparatus and run into either left or right arm
randomly. The trial ended when the mouse ate the reward pellets at the
end of the arm and went back to the entry of Y-maze. Ten second after
“sample run” trial, the mice would have a “choice run” trial. The reward pellets would only be placed at the end of the arm where the
mouse previously visited. For example, if the mouse ran into the left
arm in the “sample run” trial, it would be rewarded when it still ran into
the left arm in the “choice run” trial. And we considered it as a “correct

choice”. In the ‘choice run’ trial, the chance level of choice rate is 50%
and mice with correct choice rate of at least 80% in the training session
were qualified for the following testing session.

The mice were given 10 testing sessions per day after finishing
magnetic field exposure for that day. In testing session, only “choice
run” trial was performed. The correct choice rate and the latency for
each mouse were recorded, respectively. The mean correct choice rate
was calculated by averaging the correct choices of each mouse in each
group. An infrared sensor was equipped at the intersection of the Y
maze to detect the occurrence and record the time duration of one

single trial. The mean latency was calculated by averaging the time for
each mouse in each group to complete one trial. In a testing session, as
soon as the mice came into the decision-making point for left-right
discrimination in Y-maze, the time point was detected by the infrared
sensor, a “reference point (RP)” was set for data analysis.

2.5. Data recording and analysis

2.5.1. LFPs and spikes recording
8-channel LFPs and spikes were recorded simultaneously from the
hippocampus of mice during above behavior testing by Cerebus
Acqusition System (Cyberkinetics, Utah, USA). LFPs were amplified
(gain: 4000), filtered (0–250 Hz) and sampled at 2000 Hz. Meanwhile,
spikes (high pass filter: 0.25–5 kHz, sampled at 30 kHz) with power
of > 5 times the standard deviation from the baseline mean were ex
tracted and stored. In present study, we recorded 105 datasets for each
group (15 for one mouse) per day for 7 consecutive days [20]. Spikes
were identified and denoised by a clustering software wave_clus [21].
Neurons firing rate was obtained at bin width of 0.05 s.

2.5.2. Time-frequency spectral analysis
Recorded LFPs were decomposed by Daubechies wavelet with order
9 and baseline drifts were removed. Various typical frequencies, including delta (0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–16 Hz), beta
(16–30 Hz), and gamma (30–60 Hz) band, were obtained through LFPs
processing after a 9-octave wavelet transform. And spectral analysis
was used to assess the different frequencies band in the LFPs during the
task. To illustrate the temporal modulation of theta and gamma band
power, the corresponding LFP spectrogram was calculated using multitaper spectral analysis (0.5 s window, 0.25 s step).

entropy

2.6. Histological analysis

After 7-day exposure under various magnetic field conditions, mice
from each group were decapitated and their brains were harvested.
Both right and left cerebral hemispheres were collected. Brain tissues
were fixed with 10% formalin, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series
and then embedded in paraffin wax. Thin cross-sections (5 μm thick)
were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) to examine
cell morphology under microscope.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (SPSS
Statistics 19.0 software, IBM SPSS Institute, USA) by ANOVA and t-test.

3. Results

The effects of various magnetic field exposures on the behavioral
performance of mice in Y-maze test are presented in Fig. 2. Clearly,
compared to SMF and SHAM group, mice in EMF group showed a significantly lower percentage of correct responses. After 3-day EMF exposure, the correct choice rate declined to 80%, whereas no obvious
decrement was observed in the mice exposed to SMF (Post hoc test,
p - 0.05). For the latency time shown in Fig. 2B, two-way repeated
measures ANOVA indicated a significant effect of group (F = 8.01,
p < 0.01) and no significant effect of time (F = 1.95, p - 0.05). The
mean latency of the mice decreased throughout exposure days in all
groups, while EMF group presents a steady tendency to drop, with a
latency time (12.9 ± 1.5 s) almost twice as long as that in SHAM
group (7.1 ± 0.6 s) after 7-day exposure (Post hoc test, p < 0.01).

As shown in Fig. 3A–C, compared to the other two groups, firing
rate of mice during Y-maze test were significantly decreased in EMF
group, in which mice were exposed to EMF. And the progressive decrease of firing rate in the EMF group shows a time-dependent feature,
with the lowest firing rate observed in Day 7 after EMF exposure. In
consideration of the physiological variation of different mice and for
better data analysis, the firing rates in each group were normalized to
obtain a mean value between 0 and 1. As shown in Fig. 3D, the quantitative data has shown that firing rate of mice during behavior testing
significantly declined after EMF exposure. The mean value of firing rate
was steeply decreased from Day 3, and continued stepping down for the
following 4 days. In the contrary, average firing rate of mice in the
other two groups remained almost steady during the entire experimental process, with a slight decreasing trend found in SMF group.
Two-way ANOVA analysis also revealed a statistically significant effect
of group (F = 25.64, p < 0.001) and a significant effect of day
(F = 7.39, p < 0.05).


Subsequently, we studied the coupling patterns between theta and

cognitive function and its ensuing behavior, and contributes to the
execution of reference memory, spatial memory and working memory

[26,27,28]. Recent studied have suggested that the strength of PAC may
change depending on cognitive demands [29]. PAC possibly occurs in

intensity of PAC. However, by simply calculating MI, it is difficult to
study the time-dependent response of PAC. Herein, in order to achieve
PAC with higher temporal resolution, the function of “Kullback-Leibler”
distance was selected and specific parameters for window and step were
applied to modify the algorithm for MI calculation [30,31]. In our
study, we have found that the MI value of theta and gamma band
peaked before mice reached the RP in all trials. Its changing tendency is
similar to that in firing rate, which indicated that neuronal spikes and
oscillations could be closely related and influence afterward memory
retrieval and decision-making results.

Based on our study, the mean firing rate and the theta-gamma of
Dur MI value were positive related (correlation coefficient = 0.901,
p < 0.01, t-test) in EMF group. And a decreased mean firing rate was
reliably accompanied by a decline in MI after exposed to EMF.
Meanwhile, both firing rate and theta-gamma MI show a clear timedependent feature during behavior performance. Our study thus


suggests that, besides decreased neuron spikes, the interruption of theta
and gamma coupling could be another contributor to EMF exposurerelated working memory deficits. And it is proposed that the delicate
time-dependent feature between spikes firing and theta-gamma coupling modulation could be necessary for information processing in WM
tasks. Therefore, one of possible electrophysiological mechanisms underlying EMF-induced WM impairment could be the abnormal temporal
correlation between neuronal spikes and oscillations [43,44].

Another interesting finding is that the effect of low frequency EMF
may be associated with duration of exposure over days. Prolonged exposure to ELF-EMF may alter various regulatory mechanisms related to
cognitive function, including protein expression inhibition [36],
transmitter disorder [13], pathological damages, and abnormal behavior [30]. Indeed, we have found that, WM task performance is in good
accordance with the progressive inhibition between spikes and neuronal oscillations versus exposure days. The longer the mice exposed to
low frequency EMF, the more significantly their WM would be influenced. Although we have observed the effects of low frequency EMF on
morphological changes in hippocampus cells, the changes of neural
cells phenotype need to be further verified. And further studies need to
