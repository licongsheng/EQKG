A B S T R A C T

Although numerous studies have reported the influence of extremely low frequency magnetic field
(ELF-MF) exposure on human health, its effects on cognitive deficits in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) have
remained under debate. Moreover, the influence of ELF-MF on hyperphosphorylated tau, which is one of
the most common pathological hallmarks of AD, has not been reported to date. Therefore, transgenic
mice (3xTg) were used in the present study. 3xTg mice, which express an APP/PS1 mutation combined
with a tau (P301L) mutation and that develop cognitive deficits at 6 months of age, were subjected to
ELF-MF (50 Hz, 500 m T) exposure or sham exposure daily for 3 months. We discovered that ELF-MF
exposure ameliorated cognitive deficits and increased synaptic proteins in 3xTg mice. The protective
effects of ELF-MF exposure may have also been caused by the inhibition of apoptosis and/or decreased
oxidative stress levels that were observed in the hippocampus tissues of treated mice. Furthermore, tau
hyperphosphorylation was decreased in vivo because of ELF-MF exposure, and this decrease was induced
by the inhibition of GSK3 b and CDK5 activities and activation of PP2Ac. We are the first to report that
exposure to ELF-MF can attenuate tau phosphorylation. These findings suggest that ELF-MF exposure
could act as a valid therapeutic strategy for ameliorating cognitive deficits and attenuating tau
hyperphosphorylation in AD.

The rapidly increased probability and duration of extremely low
frequency electromagnetic fields (ELF-EMF) exposure have become
hot topics of worldwide concern (Antonini et al., 2006). ELF-EMF is
a combination of an electric field and magnetic field. An extremely
low frequency magnetic field (ELF-MF) has a frequency that ranges
from 1 Hz to 300 Hz. When humans or animals are close to an

ELF-MF, an electric current is induced in the body (International
Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation, 2010). Although it is
known that the ELF-MF current is too weak to break molecular

bonds or to heat tissues (Feychting et al., 2005), its effects on
human health remain unknown.

In 2002, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)
classified ELF-MF exposure as a “possibly carcinogenic” factor
(Cancer, 2002). Therefore, a steadily increasing number of studies

have focused on the effects of ELF-MF exposure on human health;
however, definitive conclusions have not been drawn. In 2010, the
International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection
(ICNIRP) established a limitation for public exposure to ELF-MF in
the range of 100–200 m T (Ahlbom et al., 1998; International
Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation, 2010). Recently, we
showed that exposure to 100 m T ELF-MF had no effect on
hematology or serum chemistry in rats (Lai et al., 2016). However,
the effects of ELF-MF exposure on human diseases, especially
neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), still
require further investigation.

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and is the most
common cause of dementia among the elderly. The neuropathological hallmarks of AD are amyloid b -peptide (A b ) plaques,
neurofibrillary tangles and neurodegeneration, the latter of which
eventually leads to dementia (Goedert, 1993). Hyperphosphorylated microtubule associated tau is the major protein component of
neurofibrillary tangles. Although studies have suggested that the
number of tau tangles is positively correlated with the degree of
dementia in AD patients (Giannakopoulos et al., 1997), noninvasive and effective treatments to decrease tangles are still
lacking.

A small number of studies have focused on the effects of ELF-MF

exposure on AD, and both cognitive-protective and cognitivedeficit effects have been observed. Sobel and Davanipour have
suggested that ELF-MF exposure might increase peripheral A b
deposition and A b production in the brain by modulating Ca [2+]

channels (Sobel and Davanipour, 1996). However, Liu et al. have
reported that ELF-MF (50 Hz, 400 m T) exposure for 60 d provided
cognitive benefits (Liu et al., 2015). The above studies suggest that
the effects of ELF-MF exposure on AD require further investigation.

Interestingly, human clinical studies have observed that ELF-MF
exposure induces a significantly decreased wound depth and pain
intensity in patients with venous ulcers (Stiller et al., 1992).
Further, ELF-MF exposure experiments using a Huntington’s
disease (HD) rat model (60 Hz, 0.7 mT, 4 h for 21 days) have
shown neuroprotective effects with decreased oxidative stress
(Tunez et al., 2006a; Tunez et al., 2006b). Oxidative stress,
especially the radical pair mechanism, plays an important role in
tau pathogenesis and the progression of AD; therefore, it is of
interest to determine whether ELF-MF treatment can protect
against oxidative stress in vivo.

To explore whether ELF-MF exposure influences cognitive
ability and/or hyperphosphorylated tau levels in AD, transgenic
mice (3xTg) were used that express an APP/PS1 mutation
combined with a tau (P301L) mutation (Oddo et al., 2003).
Transgenic mice and non-transgenic 129/C57BL/6 mice (N-Tg) at
3 months of age were exposed to 500 m T of ELF-MF or to the
standard environment (SE) for 3 months. We found that ELF-MF
treatment ameliorated cognitive deficits in 3xTg mice, which
involved increasing the levels of several synaptic proteins,
increasing the neuron number, and decreasing oxidative stress.
We also found that ELF-MF exposure decreased hyperphosphorylated tau levels by increasing protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A)
activity and decreasing both glycogen synthase kinase-3 b
(GSK-3 b ) and cyclin-dependent protein kinase 5 (CDK5) activities.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Animals

Mice in this study were bred in the Experimental Animal Center
of Tongji Medical College at the Huazhong University of Science
and Technology. The 3xTg mice, which were purchased from
Jackson Laboratory, are a classical AD model that has been
described previously (Oddo et al., 2003). Briefly, these mice harbor


a knock-in of the Swedish double mutation of amyloid precursor
protein (APPKM670/671NL), a presenilin 1 mutation (PS1M146V),
and a frontotemporal dementia tau mutation (tauP301L) and are
bred on a 129/C57BL/6 background. After weaning and genotyping,
only male healthy 3xTg and non-transgenic (N-Tg) mice were
selected for this study. All mice were maintained on a 12 h dark and
12 h light cycle and were provided ad libitum chow and water
access. All animal experiments were performed according to the
“Policies on the Use of Animals and Humans in Neuroscience

Research” and were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College at the Huazhong University of Science
and Technology. The 3-month-old animals were randomly divided
into the following groups: N-Tg-SE (N-Tg mice that were exposed
only to the standard environment), N-Tg-MF (N-Tg mice that were
exposed to ELF-MF), 3xTg-SE (3xTg mice that were exposed only to
the standard environment), and 3xTg-MF (3xTg mice that were
exposed to ELF-MF), with a total of six to ten mice per group.

2.2. ELF-MF exposure

The exposure system consisted of an exposure device and a
plastic rack. Both were made with ABS plastics (Yite electric,
Wuhan, China) that are firm and insulated. There were five layers
in the exposure device (200 cm � 70 cm � 200 cm, L � W � H).
Inside the exposure device, there was a plastic rack (140 cm � 70

cm � 200 cm, L � W � H) with four floors. Bronze wires
(Yite electric, Wuhan, China) were used to transmit current and
to generate the ELF-MF. The bronze wires were affixed tightly to
the framework to decrease vibration and noise. When the system
was powered on, the sound and temperature generated were
similar to those of the control environment. In the present study,
we constructed two systems; one was used for ELF-MF exposure
and the other was used for sham exposure. For each system, there
were two beams of bronze wires in the framework. For the ELF-MF

exposure group, the two beams were oriented in the same
direction (current direction), but the beams were in opposite
directions for the sham exposure group. The device for the ELF-MF
exposure group produced a uniform ELF-MF, but the control device
did not produce an ELF-MF. Further, the two systems were located
in separate and nonadjacent rooms that had the same conditions
and that were without additional electronic devices (e.g., computers). The daylight lamps were also not in close proximity to the
system. An alternating current power supply (220 V/50 Hz) and a
TDGC2 transformer (Mushidq, Shanghai, China) were used. The
voltage of the exposure device was adjusted to 150 V, which
maintained the intensity of the ELF-MF at a frequency of 50 Hz at
500 m T, while the resulting electric field was very weak and
negligible (similar to the background level). Moreover, the
distribution of the ELF-MF was determined with a Narda
efa-300 field analyzer (Narda, Pfullingen, Germany).

The mice were exposed to the ELF-MF for 3 months for 20 h per
day. The exposure system was shut down at 8 am and restarted at
12 pm daily to protect the animal breeder.

2.3. Morris water maze

To assess spatial learning and memory, the Morris water maze
task was conducted after the exposure period as described
previously (Blaney et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2013; Morris, 1984).
Briefly, an elevated circular pool (110-cm diameter, 60 cm high)
was filled with opaque water, and the temperature was maintained
at 24–26 [�] C. Several visual cues, including a triangle, quadrilateral,
and circle of different colors, were affixed on the inner wall of the
pool, and a black curtain surrounding the pool eliminated
competing environmental cues. Mice were trained to find a hidden
platform (8-cm diameter, 30 cm high) that was 1 cm below the

water, and the training occurred for six consecutive days with four
trials per day. In the training stage, mice were given 60 s to find and
escape onto the platform; however, if they failed, the mice were
guided to the platform and were allowed to remember the location
of the platform for 15 s. The swimming pathway and latency in
locating the hidden platform was recorded by a Noldus video
tracking system. After the training phase, the platform was
removed, and the mice were monitored for a 60 s free swim in
the pool. The time spent in the target quadrant and the number of
crossings of the platform position was recorded to measure
learning and memory in the mice. All tests were conducted in the
morning from 9 am to 3 pm, and the animals in the four groups
were tested alternately to minimize the impact caused by testing at
different times of day.

2.4. Fear conditioning test

Twenty-four hours after the completion of the Morris water
maze task, mice were subjected to the fear conditioning test as
previously described (Jiang et al., 2015). During the training phase,
the animals were placed in the conditioning chamber, which was
made of white walls, for 5 min and then were subjected to 3 min of
unsignaled foot shocks (one shock at the first min, three shocks at
the second min and 8 shocks at the third min; 0.5 mA, 2 s duration,
and 1 min apart). The freezing behavior time percentage was
recorded. After the last shock, mice remained in the chamber for
1 min and were then returned to their home cages. During the
testing phase, the mice were placed back into the same
conditioning chamber without foot shock for 5 min. The freezing
behavior time percentage was recorded. At the end of each session,
mice were returned to their home cages, and the chambers were
cleaned with water after wiping with 70% ethanol. All experiments
were conducted using a video tracking system to measure the
activity and freezing behavior of the animals.

3. Results

3.1. ELF-MF exposure ameliorated cognitive deficits in 3xTg mice

After 3 months of exposure to ELF-MF or sham exposure, no
differences were observed in appearance, movement or weight
when comparing between groups (data not shown). The groups
were subjected to the Morris water maze task to detect spatial
learning and memory abilities. In the learning stage, 3xTg-SE mice
spent a longer time finding the platform than did N-Tg-SE mice
(Fig. 1A), which indicated deficits in spatial learning ability in 3xTg
mice, as previously reported (Billings et al., 2007; Pietropaolo et al.,
2008). The performance of the 3xTg-MF group was better during
the learning phase wherein they took less time to find the platform
than did the 3xTg-SE group. Similar results were also observed
during the testing phase. The mean platform crossing times of
3xTg-SE mice were decreased compared with those of N-Tg-SE
mice, but increased following ELF-MF treatment (Fig. 1B);
however, all the mice of the four groups spent the same time in
the third quadrant (Fig. 1C).

3.2. ELF-MF exposure increased memory-related protein levels in 3xTg
mice

To investigate the underlying mechanisms of the ameliorated
cognitive deficits following ELF-MF exposure, we performed
western blot analyses to detect the relative expression of
various synaptic proteins. We found that in the hippocampus of
3xTg mice, the relative levels of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor
1 (NR1) and N-methyl- D -aspartate receptor 2A (NR2A) were
increased after ELF-MF exposure (Fig. 2A and B). Increased levels
of glutamate receptor 2 (GLUR2) and postsynaptic density
protein 95 (PSD95) proteins were also observed in hippocampus
tissues of 3xTg mice (Fig. 2A and B). There were no significant
differences in the levels of N-methyl- D -aspartate receptor 2B
(NR2B), glutamate receptor 1 (GLUR1) or postsynaptic density
protein 93 (PSD93) in 3xTg mice compared to N-Tg mice (Fig. 2A
and B). We discovered that expression of NR2A and NR2B was
increased in N-Tg-MF mice compared with that in N-Tg-SE mice


(Fig. 2A and B). We also detected the mRNA levels of the same
synaptic proteins by RT-PCR. Compared to 3xTg-SE mice, the
levels of GLUR2, NR1 and PSD95 mRNA were increased after
ELF-MF exposure (Fig. 2C).

3.3. ELF-MF exposure prevented neurodegeneration by inhibiting
apoptosis in the hippocampus

stages of AD (Whitehouse et al., 1981, 1982). Therefore, we
examined the number of neurons in the CA3 and dentate gyrus

number of NeuN-positive cells in the CA3 area deceased in 3xTg-SE
mice compared with that in N-Tg-SE mice (Fig. 3A); however, this
decline was rescued greatly following ELF-MF treatment.

after ELF-MF treatment. As shown in Fig. 3B, TUNEL immunostaining in the hippocampus was enhanced in 3xTg-SE mice
compared with that of N-Tg-SE mice. A decrease in the focal
positive staining was also apparent in the 3xTg-MF group.
Moreover, we found that the expression of Bax was increased
significantly in the 3xTg-SE group but was attenuated after ELF-MF
exposure. Further, compared to the N-Tg-SE group, the expression
of Bcl-2 was decreased in the 3xTg-SE group; however, ELF-MF

hyperphosphorylated tau that emerge in the somatodendritic

2003). Further, the neurofibrillary tangles that are induced by
hyperphosphorylated tau at 9–12 months of age (Giannakopoulos
et al., 1997) are known to be associated with oxidative stress (Frost
et al., 2014). Therefore, we used western blot analysis to determine
the effects of ELF-MF exposure on the levels of phosphorylated tau

phosphorylation of tau at Thr-231, Ser-262, Ser-396 and Ser404 was significantly elevated in 3xTg-SE mice compared with that

changes were also observed in the levels of total tau that were
detected with the TAU-5 antibody (Fig. 5A and B). Furthermore, the

observed in the hippocampus following ELF-MF exposure (Fig. S2).

To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms of the ELF-MF
induced decrease in hyperphosphorylated tau, the abundance of
GSK-3 b, PP2A and CDK5 proteins were measured. We observed
that the levels of Ser-9-phosphorylated GSK3 b (an inactive form)
were increased in the 3xTg-MF group compared with the levels in
the 3xTg-SE group; however, Tyr-307-phosphorylated PP2Ac (an
inactive form) was reduced after ELF-MF exposure (Fig. 5D and E).
Moreover, the levels of p25 and p35 (the neuronal activators of
CDK5) were decreased in the hippocampus of the ELF-MF groups
compared with the 3xTg-SE group, but were similar to the levels
observed in the N-Tg-SE group (Fig. 5F and G). Further, the total
levels of GSK3 b, PP2Ac and CDK5 were not altered in any of the
animal groups. These results indicate that the effects of ELF-MF
exposure on the tau hyperphosphorylation may be caused by
inhibition of GSK3 b and CDK5 and activation of PP2Ac.

4. Discussion

To investigate the effects of ELF-MF exposure on AD in the
present study, transgenic mice (3xTg) were used that had an
APP/PS1 mutation combined with a tau (P301L) mutation. This
mouse model is known to mimic the progress of AD pathology


better than mouse models that express only tau or APP mutations
alone. It has been previously shown that 3xTg mice develop
cognitive deficits as early as 6 months of age and exhibit worse
performance on the water maze test compared to wild-type
controls (Billings et al., 2005; Clinton et al., 2007; Pietropaolo et al.,
2008). The 3xTg mouse model is also characterized by A b
aggregation. Further, although neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are
absent until 12 months of age, hyperphosphorylated tau is
detectable and elevated after 6 months of age (Hradek et al.,
2015; Mastrangelo and Bowers, 2008). Herein, we are the first to
report that exposure to ELF-MF can ameliorate tau hyperphosphorylation and improve cognitive capabilities.

3xTg mice showed impaired learning and memory capabilities
compared with N-Tg mice in the Morris water maze and fear
conditioning tests, and this was ameliorated by ELF-MF exposure.
Moreover, the level of phosphorylated tau was increased
dramatically in 3xTg mice in the standard environment. Additionally, A b aggregation, synaptic damage and apoptosis were all
increased in 3xTg mice, but all of these changes were reversed by
ELF-MF exposure.

It has been reported that 3xTg mice show impaired synaptic
function at 6 months of age (Chen et al., 2014; Pratt et al., 2011), and
this has been further confirmed in our present study. The levels of
synaptic proteins, including NR1, NR2A, GLUR2 and PSD95, were
increased in 3xTg-MF mice compared with the levels in 3xTg-SE
mice. The aforementioned synaptic proteins are thought to be
important in learning and memory processes. For example,
NR1 and NR2 are two subtypes of the NMDA receptor, which is
known to take part in memory formation and storage (Cui et al.,
2004; Goeldner et al., 2008). Our work also suggests that loss of
neurons in the hippocampus can be rescued by inhibition of
apoptosis that occurs after ELF-MF exposure. Together, these
changes might be responsible for the improved cognitive
capabilities of 3xTg-MF mice and may also be a result of the
observed decreased tau hyperphosphorylation that occurs after
ELF-MF exposure.

Herein, we are the first to explore the relationship between
ELF-MF exposure and tau hyperphosphorylation, which is one of
the cardinal pathological features of AD. Intraneuronal hyperphosphorylated tau tangles can impair neuronal survival and
working memory (Grundke-Iqbal et al., 1986; Li et al., 2007). The
current study shows that 3xTg-SE mice develop increased levels of
phosphorylated tau that are decreased following ELF-MF exposure.
Moreover, these findings were confirmed herein by analyzing the
levels of several upstream kinases that have been reported to
regulate tau pathology, including GSK-3 b, CDK5 and PP2Ac (Tian
and Wang, 2002; Wang et al., 2007).

The mechanisms underlying learning and memory are very
complicated, and previous studies have discovered that the
synaptic protein levels (Yang et al., 2008), neuron number
(Whitehouse et al., 1981), oxidative stress (Revel et al., 2015),
phosphorylated tau protein levels (Sydow et al., 2011) and A b
deposition (Lim et al., 2014) are all involved in cognitive ability.
Many studies have shown that both A b accumulation and
increased phosphorylated tau protein levels induce cognitive
deficits in various AD mouse models, including the Tg2576 model
(APPswe) (Dineley et al., 2002), the 3xTg model (APPSwe,
PS1M146V, and tauP301L) (Clinton, Billings, 2007), and the 5xFAD
model (APPswe/lnd/fl and a PS1 transgene carrying double FAD
mutations (M146L and L286V)) (Ohno et al., 2007). In the present
study, we have detected reductions in both A b accumulation and

Others have reported that ELF-MF treatment can ameliorate A b
deposition (Liu et al., 2015), which was also observed in our current
study. ELF-MF treatment can also ameliorate hyperphosphorylated
tau; however, the relationship between tau and A b in AD is
unclear. Some studies have suggested that A b is the initial
pathological feature of AD and that tau pathology is the product of
A b aggregation (Hardy, 2006; Hardy and Higgins, 1992); however,
it has also been suggested that tau is independent of A b in AD
(Kopke et al., 1993). Therefore, it is of interest to determine
whether tau hyperphosphorylation is affected by altering A b
accumulation. In the present study, both A b deposition and
hyperphosphorylated tau were decreased in vivo after ELF-MF
treatment, which could potentially contribute to decreasing both
apoptosis and oxidative stress (Takagane et al., 2015; Naini and
Soussi-Yanicostas, 2015).

Further, our work also indicated that ELF-MF exposure
decreased the levels of ROS in the brain, which were markedly
increased in 3xTg-SE mice. Tasset et al. reported that ELF-MF
(0.7 mT) exposure for 21 days had neuroprotective effects on
oxidative stress and cell damage via activation of the Nrf2
antioxidant response pathway (Tasset et al., 2013). Herein, ELFMF had a protective effect on oxidative stress, which has also been
shown to play an important role in the pathogenesis of hyperphosphorylated tau (Frost et al., 2014; Nunomura et al., 1999).
Additionally, others have shown that tau is phosphorylated at
multiple sites by GSK-3 b and CDK5 in AD and is dephosphorylated
by PP2A (Wang et al., 2007), the latter of which can be triggered by
oxidative stress (Keeney et al., 2012). Therefore, the protective
effects of ELF-MF on oxidative stress may be the underlying
mechanism for the reduction of hyperphosphorylated tau observed in the mice exposed to ELF-MF treatment.

Other groups have suggested that exposure to ELF-MF might
increase AD risk. A study that was carried out by Trimmel and
Schweiger has shown that exposure to 50 Hz and 1 mT ELF-MF
caused a reduction of cognitive performance in rats (Trimmel and
Schweiger, 1998). However, our previous work and studies by
others have alleged that ELF-MF exposure has no effect on AD or
cognitive ability (Zhang et al., 2015). The differences in these
findings may be due to different technical parameters (e.g.,
differences in exposure intensity). Most of the studies have
reported that there are negative effects when flux intensities
exceeding 1 mT are used. In the present study, we used 500 m T as
the exposure intensity, which is moderate because the limitation
for public or occupational exposure is 200 m T or 1 mT, respectively.

Notwithstanding, there are still issues that require further
investigation. First, although we discovered that ELF-MF exposure
decreased the production of ROS, the effects of ELF-MF on the
elimination of ROS remain unknown. Second, it is unknown
whether ELF-MF exposure can have beneficial effects in AD animal
models that develop severe cognitive deficits and neurofibrillary
tangles. Thus, we intend to pursue studies to address these
questions.

5. Conclusion

In summary, we discovered that long-term ELF-MF treatment
ameliorates cognitive deficits and tau hyperphosphorylation by
increasing synaptic protein levels and decreasing oxidative stress
in young 3xTg AD mice. We are also the first to report that ELF-MF
treatment can decrease tau hyperphosphorylation, which may be a
new means to cure AD.
