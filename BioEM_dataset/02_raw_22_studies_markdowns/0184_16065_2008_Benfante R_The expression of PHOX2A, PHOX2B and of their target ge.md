1. Introduction


a b s t r a c t

The homeodomain transcription factors PHOX2A and PHOX2B are vital for development of the autonomic
nervous system. Their spatial and temporal expression at the neural crest is instrumental in determining
neuronal precursor fate, and by regulating DbH expression, the enzyme catalysing noradrenaline synthesis from dopamine, they also play a role in determination of noradrenergic phenotype. Disturbing this
finely regulated process leads to disruption of autonomic development and autonomic dysfunction syndromes such as DbH deficiency. As it had previously been shown that the catecholamine system is
responsive to ELF-EMF, and as this has also been linked to various pathologies and to certain types of cancer, we wondered whether exposure to this type of radiation could affect the expression of PHOX2A,
PHOX2B and DbH, also during differentiation triggered by retinoic acid. To investigate this possibility
we exposed the human SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell line to 50 Hz power-line magnetic field at various
flux densities and for various exposure times. We measured gene expression in exposed cells compared
to control cells and also investigated any changes at protein level. Using our exposure protocol, we found
no changes at either transcript or protein level of these important components of the autonomic nervous
system and catecholaminergic system.

neuronal progenitors at the neural crest and in determination of
noradrenergic phenotype (Stanke et al., 1999; Lo et al., 1999). In
the peripheral nervous system they are found in all autonomic
ganglia (sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric) as well as
some ganglia of cranial and vagal nerves. In the central nervous

system they are found in all noradrenergic and adrenergic neurons
which also express dopamine-b-hydroxylase (DbH), a mono-oxygenase that catalyses the synthesis of noradrenaline from dopamine. DbH is also expressed in large quantities in chromaffin
granules of the adrenal medulla. Both PHOX2A and PHOX2B are
able to activate the DbH promoter (Kim et al., 1998; Yang et al.,
1998) and there now exists a variety of studies showing that these
transcription factors are important in determining noradrenergic
phenotype, with loss of function of PHOX2A leading to agenesis
of the locus coeruleus, the main noradrenergic centre of the brain
(Morin et al., 1997; Hirsch et al., 1998). PHOX2B knockout phenotype is more severe and causes failure of development of all components of the autonomic nervous system (Pattyn et al., 1999).

During the specification of neuronal identity, control of temporal and spatial expression of PHOX2A and PHOX2B is fundamental
(Brunet and Pattyn, 2002), and many studies over the last few

years have tried to elucidate the exact molecular mechanisms involved in regulation of their expression (Flora et al., 2001; Cargnin
et al., 2005). The process is a finely tuned cooperation between
extrinsic factors and so-called intrinsic factors such as the here

mentioned PHOX2 transcription factors. Disturbing this highly
organised process disrupts development and also results in autonomic dysfunction. A clinical example of autonomic dysfunction
is dopamine-b-hydroxylase (DbH) deficiency, a rare autosomal
recessive disorder which was shown to result from mutation of

the DbH gene (Kim et al., 2002; Deinum et al., 2004). The mutation
causes altered expression of this catecholamine-synthesising enzyme and the resulting condition includes cardiovascular disorders
and orthostatic hypotension (Senard and Rouet, 2006; Robertson et
al., 1991). Severe orthostatic syndrome is a consequence of total
absence of circulating adrenaline and noradrenaline and increased
plasma dopamine. The syndrome is characterised by hypotension,
muscle hypotonia, hypothermia and hypoglycaemia, symptoms
which present early in childhood and worsen progressively into
adolescence limiting greatly the activity of those affected. Mutations in the coding sequence of both PHOX2A and PHOX2B have also
been shown to be associated with various pathologies. Polyalanine
expansions and frameshift mutations of the PHOX2B gene are
linked to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS or
Ondine’s curse), a potentially fatal respiratory disorder characterised by absence of autonomic control of breathing (Amiel et al.,
2003; Borghini et al., 2006; Sasaki et al., 2003). Mutations in the
PHOX2A gene on the other hand are known to cause congenital
fibrosis of the extraocular muscles type 2 (CFEOM2), a condition
in which patients demonstrate restrictive opthalmoplegia, bilateral
congenital exotropia and severe ptosis caused by lack of development of the oculomotor and trochlear nerves (Bosley et al., 2006;
Nakano et al., 2001).

Extremely low-frequency electromagnetic fields or ELF-EMF are
a type of non-ionising radiation arising from the flow of electricity
in overhead power-lines. The electric and magnetic fields of 50 Hz
measured beneath energised power transmission lines are generally in the ranges of 1–10 kV m [�][1] and 1–10 l T, respectively, but
can reach 11 kV m [�][1] and 100 l T (Lacy-Hulbert et al., 1998). ELFEMF has caused increasing concern for the inhabitants of populated areas in the vicinity of electrical power-lines, as it has been
speculated that they may be at risk from detrimental health effects,
and has thus become the subject of debate and investigation over
recent years. A relationship between ELF-EMF and health risks was
initially presented by a child leukaemia study in 1979 (Wertheimer
and Leeper, 1979) and has since been studied extensively with
reports suggesting the possibility of a link to neurodegenerative
diseases such as Alzheimer’s (Sobel et al., 1995, 1996; Sobel and
Davanipour, 1996) and certain types of cancer including leukaemia
and brain tumors (McKinney, 2005; Lacy-Hulbert et al., 1998;
Johansen, 2000; Ahlbom, 2001; Feychting et al., 2003; Hakansson
et al., 2003). No studies however have been carried out to determine if there is a greater incidence of the fore mentioned autonomic dysfunction syndromes within populations of people
living close to power-lines. The idea of genotoxicity and the possibility that ELF-EMF could be responsible for mutagenesis within
the DNA sequence has also been studied, but reviews of the evidence are unsupportive of this, as many of the studies carried
out were unable to provide conclusive results due to problems of
conflicting data and incapacity to reproduce findings between different laboratories. It has been shown however, that this type of
magnetic field is able to modify the catecholamine system which
has been studied by exposure of chromaffin and PC12 cells to
ELF-EMF (Verdugo-Diaz et al., 1998; Opler et al., 1997). It was seen
that ELF-EMF was able to cause differentiation of chromaffin cells
thus suggesting the ability to alter gene expression
(Olivares-Banuelos et al., 2004).


As we had already investigated the effect of ELF-EMF on the
expression of various subunits of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (Antonini et al., 2006), we asked ourselves if the same exposures to ELF-EMF could affect the expression of the PHOX2
transcription factors or DbH, thus leading to a disturbed regulation
of the spatial and temporal balance of expression of these factors,
causing some of the problems mentioned previously such as defective development or autonomic dysfunction. With these considerations in mind, we set about analysing the expression of PHOX2A,
PHOX2B and DbH as a result of exposure to ELF-EMF in the neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Cell lines and cultures

The SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line (Biedler et al.,
1973) was kindly provided by Dr. E. Sher (Passafaro et al., 1992).
The cells were grown in RPMI-1640, 10% fetal calf serum,
100 units/ml penicillin, 100 l g/ml streptomycin, and 2 mM L -glutamine at 37 �C and 5% CO 2, and were plated one day before exposure at densities depending on the exposure protocol: 2.6 � 10 [6]

cells per 100 mm dish for 16 h exposure; 2 � 10 [6] cells per
100 mm dish for 48 h exposure followed by immediate cell recovery; and 1 � 10 [6] cells per 100 mm dish when the cells were exposed for 48 h and harvested 48 h after the end of the exposure.
The aim of this was to ensure the collection of the same amount

of cells, which were 80% confluent, at the end of the different exposure protocols.

For all differentiation experiments, All-Trans-Retinoic Acid
(ATRA; Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA), dissolved in
100% EtOH, was added at a 10 l M final concentration. SH-SY5Y
were plated the day before being stimulated using the density
guides previously described and ATRA added to fresh cell
medium immediately before exposure to ELF-EMF. For the longer
differentiation experiments, SH-SY5Y were maintained in the
presence of 10 l M ATRA or EtOH for 10 days with medium
changes every other day. Control and EtOH treated cells were
passed at various points during the 10 days to ensure their confluency never exceeded 80%. ATRA treated SH-SY5Y were never
passed, as during differentiation, proliferation gradually slowed
and 80% confluency was never exceeded. At the end of the
10 day differentiation period, cells were exposed to ELF-EMF for
a further 48 h. Cells were collected within an hour of the end

of exposure for all experiments, pelleted at 1000 rpm, 4 �C for
5 min, and following washing with 1� PBS, divided into two
for both RNA and protein analysis, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen
and conserved at �80 �C until processed.

2.2. ELF-EMF exposure apparatus and protocols

The cells were exposed to a 50 Hz power-line magnetic field in a
‘‘blind trial” system that allows direct comparison with unexposed
control cells.

The detailed design and characterisation of the exposure system
has been previously described (Schuderer et al., 2004; Antonini et
al., 2006).

The different exposure protocols involved various magnetic
flux densities and exposure times: (a) 2 mT rms magnetic flux density, with intermittent exposure of 5 min on/5 min off for 16 h;
(b) 1 mT rms magnetic flux density, with intermittent exposure
of 5 min on/5 min off for 16 h; (c) 2 mT rms magnetic flux density,
continuous exposure for 16 h; (d) 1 mT rms flux density, continuous exposure for 16 h; and (e) 1 mT rms magnetic flux density,
continuous exposure for 48 h. For intermittent exposures, the

2.4. Total RNA extraction for reverse transcription and quantitative
real time PCR

For real time PCR analyses, total RNA was extracted from
exposed and sham-exposed retinoic acid (or control) treated SHSY5Y cells using the RNeasy [�] Mini Kit and accompanying
QIAshredder [TM] (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A maximum of 9 � 10 [6] cells was used for each

extraction, to avoid exceeding the columns’ limit. To avoid DNA contamination of samples, a 15 min on column incubation was carried
out with DNaseI (Qiagen). Eluted total RNA quality and quantity was
determined using UV light at 260 nm and 280 nm in a spectrophotometre, and subsequently 1 l g (50 ng/ l l) per sample was reversely
transcribed using the SuperScript TM III First-Strand Synthesis System

for RT-PCR (Invitrogen Ltd., Paisley, UK), again following the manufacturer’s instructions. For quantitative analysis of gene expression
we used the ABI Prism [�] 7000 Sequence Detection System, SDS software version 1.2.3 (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). Target sequences
were amplified from 50 ng of cDNA using a pre-programmed thermal profile of enzyme activation at 50 �C followed by 40 identical
cycles of denaturation at 95 �C for 15 s and annealing and
amplification at 60 �C for 1 min, conditions previously determined
by Applied Biosystems as optimal. TaqMan [�] Primer and Probe assays used were human PHOX2A (Hs00605931_mH), PHOX2B
(Hs00243679_m1), DbH (Hs00168025_m1) and the endogenous
control GADPH (Hs99999905_m1). For calculation of results, the
2 [�] [DD] [Ct] method was used allowing normalisation to GAPDH and
to the calibrator which is set to a value of 1.


2.5. Protein preparation and western blot analysis

Total protein extract was prepared from sham and exposed cells
by the freeze and thaw method. Briefly, cells were detached by
scraping in 1� PBS and collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm
for 15 min at 4 �C. The pellet was resuspended in 1� PBS containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma–Aldrich, St.Louis,
Missouri, USA) and 20 mM phenyl-methyl-sulphonyl-fluoride
(PMSF). Cells were lysed by four repeated passages in liquid nitrogen followed by incubation at 37 �C until thawed. NaCl, at a final
concentration of 400 mM, was subsequently added to allow extraction of nuclear protein. Samples were incubated for 10 min on ice
and extract clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at
4 �C in a table-top centrifuge (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany).
Twenty micrograms of total extract were separated by means of
10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (Schleicher & Schuell BioScience GmbH, Dassel, Germany). The membranes were pre-incubated for 1 h with blocking buffer (5% nonfat dry milk, 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween
20), after which the primary antibodies anti-PHOX2A (Benfante
et al., 2007), anti-PHOX2B (Cargnin et al., 2005), anti-DbH (Sigma–Aldrich Inc., Missouri, USA) or anti-b-tubulin (Sigma) were
added at appropriate dilutions and incubated for 2 h; the antichicken secondary antibodies (Davids Biotechnologie, Ragensburg,
Germany), or for b-tubulin anti-mouse (Pierce Biotechnology Inc.,
Rockford, USA), conjugated with horseradish peroxidase, were then
added and incubated for 1 h. After appropriate washes, the bands
were revealed using Super Signal West Dura (Pierce Biotechnology
Inc., Rockford, USA). Standard molecular weights (New England
Biolabs Inc., Beverly, USA) were loaded in parallel.

2.6. Data analysis

NIH Image 1.61/fat software was used for densitometric analysis of signals obtained from both Northern and Western blots. Results are shown as mean ± standard error (SE), unless otherwise
indicated, of at least three independent experiments. Paired twotailed Student’s t test was used for statistical analysis using the
GraphPad Prism 4 Software (GraphPad Software, Inc.), and values
of p < 0.05 were considered as significant.

3. Results

The SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell line was continuously
exposed to ELF-EMF (50 Hz, power-line signal) for 16 h at flux densities of 2 mT rms and 1 mT rms and the levels of expression of the
DbH, PHOX2A and PHOX2B genes were analysed by means of Northern blotting. Fig. 1 (panel A) shows the results obtained in one
independent experiment at 2 mT rms (lane 1) together with that of
the sham-exposed cells (lane 2). HeLa total RNA (lane 3) was used
as a negative control. Densitometric quantification of the mRNA
levels obtained from five independent experiments, did not show
any statistically significant difference in the expression of PHOX2A,
PHOX2B or DbH genes between the exposed and sham-exposed
cells at 2 mT rms (Fig. 1, panel B) and 1 mT rms (Fig. 1, panel C). We
then investigated whether intermittent exposure could affect the
expression of these genes. Once again, 16 h exposure to an intermittent magnetic field (5 min on/5 min off) at flux densities of
2 mT rms (Fig. 2, panel A) and 1 mT rms (Fig. 2, panel B), did not affect
the expression of PHOX2A, PHOX2B or DbH genes.

ELF-EMF may affect the expression of the genes encoding PHOX2A,
PHOX2B and DbH. To answer this question we used two different

For our investigation into the effects of ELF-EMF exposure on
the homeodomain transcription factors PHOX2A, PHOX2B and
mono-oxygenase DbH, we tested a variety of flux densities for various periods of exposure time using the power-line type exposure
in order to recreate the type of ELF-EMF originating from powerlines, thus representing as closely as possible a real-life exposure
situation, and also to recreate experimental conditions that had already produced effects in different biological models. Both continuous and intermittent type exposures were tested, as previous
studies of ELF-EMF had shown that skin cancer incidence in mice

exposed to intermittent type ELF-EMF was significantly higher
than those exposed to a continuous type field (Rannug et al.,
1994). We also investigated the possibility of delayed effects of
ELF-EMF, such as the disruption of second messenger cascades,
by collecting cells both immediately or 48 h after the end of exposure. We measured the relative levels of mRNA transcript for
PHOX2A, PHOX2B and DbH for exposed samples compared to
sham-exposed cells by means of Northern blot hybridisation, and
used Western blot analysis to identify any changes in PHOX2A,
PHOX2B and DbH at protein level. The results of our investigations
in the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y have shown quite
clearly that the expression of PHOX2A, PHOX2B and DbH is unaffected by exposure to non-ionising ELF-EMF under the conditions
here tested. Since these factors are involved in the neuronal differ
entiation process and indeed disturbing this highly organised process disrupts development and may also results in autonomic
dysfunction, we also tested whether ELF-EMF could interfere with
their expression here. In particular, PHOX2A protein expression is
down-regulated when cells are challenged to differentiate in the
presence of retinoic acid. Exposure to ELF-EMF however, did not
interfere with this regulatory mechanism either at the early stages
or at termination of the differentiation process. We found that the
inability of ELF-EMF to modify expression levels during ATRA treatment was also true for PHOX2B and DbH. We had previously
shown that the SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cell line, which expresses
PHOX2A, PHOX2B and DbH in abundance, is responsive to this type
of radiation. This was shown by the Human Unigene RZPD-2 cDNA
array which identified the up or down-regulation of various genes
as a result of ELF-EMF exposure (Antonini et al., 2006). We would
not therefore attribute our negative findings simply to a lack of
sensitivity of the system. A review of the current evidence of the
effects of ELF-EMF on a wide range of systems, cell types and biological processes (McCann, 1998) is also supportive against the
genotoxic effects of ELF-EMF exposure alone, especially at low flux
density (less than 5 mT rms ). There are however numerous reports
suggesting that ELF-EMF at higher flux densities could enhance
the effect of other mutagens such as X-rays (Miyakoshi et al.,
1999; Walleczek et al., 1999). Testing these types of conditions
however was beyond the scope of our experiments, although
analysis of gene expression as a result of a more complex exposure
protocol would be interesting. Our study is the first of its kind to
test the effect of ELF-EMF exposure on the expression of transcription factors PHOX2A and PHOX2B and mono-oxygenase DbH, all
important components of the autonomic nervous system, and
therefore our negative findings are an important addition to the
growing evidence that is for the most part unsupportive of
EMF-ELF affecting biological systems at the low-frequency here
tested.
