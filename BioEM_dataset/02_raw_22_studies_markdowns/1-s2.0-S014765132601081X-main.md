**1.** **Introduction**


A B S T R A C T

The widespread use of Wi-Fi-derived radiofrequency electromagnetic radiation (RF-EMR) has raised concerns
regarding male reproductive health; however, whether paternal exposure exerts transgenerational effects on
offspring development remains unclear. This study investigated the effects of chronic paternal 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi RF
–
EMR exposure (whole-body SAR 0.125 0.5 W/kg, 4 h/day, 6 days/week for 20 weeks) on sperm quality in F0
male mice and placental development in F1 offspring, with a focus on epigenetic regulation. Paternal RF-EMR
exposure significantly reduced sperm concentration and increased the sperm abnormality rate in F0 males. F1
offspring exhibited reduced fetal weight and placental efficiency, with more pronounced impairments in male
offspring. Mechanistically, paternal exposure reduced the proportion of placental labyrinth layer and downregulated multiple nutrient transporters in a male-biased manner. Epigenetic analysis revealed sex-dependent
histone modifications in placentas following paternal RF-EMR exposure: male placentas exhibited increased
H3K9me2/3, H3K27me3, H3K27ac, and H3K4me2, whereas female placentas exhibited decreased H3K4me2.
ChIP-qPCR targeting transporter promoters revealed sex-specific enrichment of repressive histone marks in
placentas from exposed fathers. In male placentas, H3K27me3 was increased at the *Atp1a1* and *Slc22a3* promoters, and H3K9me3 was increased at the *Atp1a1* and *Slc2a1* promoters. In female placentas, H3K27me3 at the

*Slc3a2*
promoter was significantly decreased. Collectively, chronic paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR exposure impairs fetal
growth and placental efficiency, with male offspring being more susceptible, and is associated with disrupted
placental structure, impaired nutrient transport, and sex-dependent histone modifications in the offspring
placenta. These findings underscore the importance of considering paternal RF-EMR exposure in reproductive
risk assessment.


Wi-Fi has become an indispensable technology in modern society,
with widespread use in both daily life and professional settings. However, the continuous emission of radiofrequency electromagnetic radiation (RF-EMR) from Wi-Fi devices has raised widespread concerns
regarding its potential environmental and health effects (Pall, 2018).
Unlike mobile phones, which primarily cause localized exposure, Wi-Fi
routers typically result in whole-body RF-EMR exposure (Jaffar et al.,
2019). Mounting epidemiological and experimental evidence indicates
that chronic exposure to Wi-Fi-derived RF-EMR is associated with


multiple adverse effects, including cognitive dysfunction, metabolic
disorders, and reproductive impairment (Pall, 2018; Sage and Burgio,
2018). Notably, the World Health Organization (WHO) commissioned
12 systematic reviews and meta-analyses to evaluate the health effects of
RF-EMR. Most of these reviews were limited by significant methodological shortcomings, including the omission of eligible studies, heterogeneity among primary studies, and inappropriate subgroup
analyses, thereby limiting their ability to reliably confirm the safety of
wireless communication devices (Melnick et al., 2025). Among these
assessments, the review of animal carcinogenicity provided
high-certainty evidence for heart schwannomas and moderate-certainty

highlight the need to optimize exposure guidelines and mitigate the
reproductive health risks associated with RF-EMR (Melnick et al., 2025).

The male reproductive system is particularly sensitive to RF-EMR
exposure, with testicular tissue exhibiting vulnerability comparable to
that of neural and dermal tissues (Vijay et al., 2025). Clinical studies
have consistently linked Wi-Fi exposure to impaired sperm quality,
characterized by reduced progressive motility and increased DNA fragmentation in human spermatozoa (Avendano et al., 2012; Ding et al.,
2018; Bektas and Dasdag, 2025). Animal studies have further demonstrated that RF-EMR disrupts the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis,
leading to decreased serum levels of follicle-stimulating hormone and
testosterone in rodents (Maluin et al., 2024; Khoshbakht et al., 2021).
Histological analyses in mice have shown that RF-EMR exposure reduces
seminiferous tubule diameter, epithelial thickness, tubule density, and
the number of Sertoli cells (Cakmak et al., 2026). Consequently, exposed
males exhibit severe spermatogenic abnormalities, including increased
morphological abnormalities and reduced sperm concentration,
motility, and viability (Vijay et al., 2025; Maluin et al., 2024; Khoshbakht et al., 2021; Dasdag et al., 2015a).

Beyond direct tissue toxicity, RF-EMR may disrupt cellular functions
through epigenetic mechanisms (Abtin et al., 2024; Giorgi and Del Re,
2021; Cantu et al., 2023). Epigenetic modifications—including DNA
methylation, histone post-translational modifications, and non-coding
RNA expression—mediate environmentally induced phenotypic
changes without altering the underlying DNA sequence (Skvortsova
et al., 2018). Such epigenetic reprogramming is highly dynamic during germ cell development (Skvortsova et al., 2018; Xavier et al., 2019).
Environmental stressors are known to disrupt epigenetic programming
in sperm, with adverse consequences for both male fertility and
offspring health (Kitamura et al., 2015; Ashapkin et al., 2023; Zhang
et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2024). However, whether RF-EMR directly
affects sperm epigenetics remains unclear. Current evidence indicates
that RF-EMR can alter epigenetic modifications in somatic cells. For
example, it alters ER α gene methylation in male Sprague-Dawley rat
tissues (Mokarram et al., 2017). It also induces global DNA methylation
changes in human keratinocytes (Cantu et al., 2023). In addition,
long-term RF-EMR exposure reduces miR-106b-5p and miR-107 levels in
the brains of adult male rats (Dasdag et al., 2015b, 2015c), and decreases H3K9me3 levels in the heads of adult Drosophila (Cappucci
et al., 2022). Although these observations do not constitute direct evidence in germ cells, they raise the possibility that RF-EMR may similarly
alter the epigenetic landscape of sperm, given the shared sensitivity of
reproductive and somatic tissues to RF-EMR (Vijay et al., 2025).
Collectively, these findings suggest that RF-EMR-induced epigenetic
alterations may be transmitted across generations through the paternal
germline, warranting further investigation.

Epidemiological studies have also linked paternal RF-EMR exposure
to adverse developmental outcomes in offspring. For example, higher
paternal exposure to high-frequency antennas is linearly associated with
a reduced male-to-female birth ratio (Baste et al., 2008). Additional
evidence links paternal RF-EMR exposure to an increased risk of neuroblastoma, preterm birth, preeclampsia, and certain congenital anomalies (De Roos et al., 2001; Mjoen et al., 2006; Blaasaas et al., 2002;
Baste et al., 2012). We previously showed that 10-week paternal
RF-EMR exposure predisposes male offspring to impaired glucose
metabolism in a mouse model (Yan et al., 2022). However, whether
chronic paternal Wi-Fi exposure influences intrauterine development
through sperm-mediated epigenetic mechanisms remains to be
determined.

To date, existing studies have primarily focused on RF-EMR-induced
sperm damage, leaving a critical gap in understanding whether chronic
paternal Wi-Fi exposure exerts intergenerational effects on placental and
fetal development. In particular, no studies have systematically


examined whether such paternal exposure leads to sex-specific placental
abnormalities or differential susceptibility between male and female
offspring. To address this gap, the present study established a 20-week
mouse model of chronic paternal exposure to 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi RF-EMR.
Our findings demonstrate for the first time that chronic paternal Wi-Fi
exposure impairs fetal and placental development in F1 offspring in a
sex-specific manner, with male offspring exhibiting more pronounced
abnormalities than females, indicating a previously unrecognized malebiased susceptibility in mice.

**2.** **Materials and methods**

*2.1.* *Experimental animals*

Six-week-old ICR mice were purchased from Beijing Huafukang
Laboratory Animal Center (Beijing, China). All animal experiments were
approved by the Ethics Committee of Animal and Medicine of Tangdu
Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No.:
SYXK2019–001). Upon arrival, mice were acclimatized for one week
under standard laboratory conditions (temperature: 21–23 [◦] C, humidity:
50–60%, 12 h light/dark cycle, ventilation frequency: 10–15 times/h)
prior to the experiment. Mice had free access to standard mouse chow
and sterile tap water throughout both the acclimatization and experimental periods.

*2.2.* *Construction of the Wi-Fi-associated RF-EMR platform*

A continuous wave (CW) signal generator (China Electronics Tech
=
nology Instruments Co., Ltd., China), an amplifier (output power
8.4 W; SWSPA-C200S/C-2, Chengdu SWIEE Power Electronics Technology Co., Ltd, China), and a horn antenna (gain: 8.94 dB, CW; LB1080, A-INFO, China) were used to establish the Wi-Fi RF-EMR exposure system. The electromagnetic radiation frequency was set at
2.4 GHz, and mice were allowed free movement within their cages
during exposure. The whole-body specific absorption rate (SAR) was
measured using a SAR meter (Narda SRM-3006, Narda-Storp, Germany)
and calculated based on a prolate spheroidal model of a medium-sized
mouse (Yan et al., 2022), yielding a whole-body SAR range of
0.125–0.5 W/kg across different positions within the exposure chamber.
The uniformity of the electromagnetic field within the exposure chamber was verified to ensure consistent exposure across all cages.

*2.3.* *Experimental Animal Grouping and Treatment*

Male mice were individually housed under the standard conditions
described above. The mice were randomly assigned to two groups
(n = 20 per group): the radiation exposure group (RE, Wi-Fi-derived RFEMR) and the sham exposure group (SE). A total of 40 mice were
included in this study. Mice in the RE group received daily RF-EMR

–
exposure (4 h/day, 9:00 13:00, with a 15-min break after 2 h of exposure to minimize stress) for 6 consecutive days, followed by 1 day of rest,
for a total of 20 weeks. Mice in the SE group were housed in the same
exposure chamber with inactive equipment (no RF-EMR emission) to
control for non-thermal effects associated with the devices, under
otherwise identical conditions as the RE group.

After 20 weeks of exposure, RE and SE male mice were mated with
unexposed female ICR mice at a ratio of 1:1. Vaginal plugs were checked
daily at 08:00, and the day on which a vaginal plug was detected was
designated as 0.5 days post-coitum (dpc). F1 offspring and their placentas were collected at 18.5 dpc for subsequent analyses.

*2.4.* *Hemocytometer-based measurement of sperm concentration in mice*

Multiple small incisions were made in the isolated cauda epididymides, which were then immersed in 100 µL of prewarmed HTF medium. The tissues were incubated at 37 [◦] C for 10 min to allow passive

Ten mice were included in each group (biological replicates, n = 10
per group). Each diluted sperm sample was loaded and counted three

*2.5.* *Diff-quik staining for morphological analysis of mouse spermatozoa*

Briefly, 10 µL of diluted sperm suspension was applied to a precleaned, degreased glass slide, and a thin monolayer smear was prepared by gently dragging the edge of a second glass slide across the
sample at a consistent angle to avoid mechanical damage to the sperm
flagella. The smear was completely air-dried at room temperature and
then fixed by immersion in 95% methanol for 2 min. After fixation,
excess methanol was allowed to drain vertically onto filter paper. The
slides were sequentially stained by immersion in Diff-Quik Solution 1

–
(eosinophilic dye) for 20 30 s, followed by Diff-Quik Solution 2 (baso
–
philic dye) for 10 15 s. After each staining step, the slides were held
vertically at a 90 [◦] angle against absorbent paper to remove excess
staining solution before proceeding to the next reagent. Excess dye was
gently removed by dipping the slides 5–10 times in distilled water
without prolonged soaking to prevent over-decolorization. The slides
were completely air-dried under ambient conditions before microscopic
examination. Sperm morphology was examined under a 100 × oil immersion objective lens (total magnification, ×1000). For each sample,
100 consecutive spermatozoa were evaluated from at least five
randomly selected microscopic fields using a systematic raster-scanning
pattern to minimize subjective counting bias. Morphological defects of
the sperm head, neck/midpiece, and flagellum were recorded
separately.

Under Diff-Quik staining, the acrosomal region of the sperm head
appears light blue, the post-acrosomal region appears dark blue, the
midpiece appears red, and the tail appears blue or light red. Sperm
morphological abnormalities were classified into five categories based
on the location of the defect (Ali et al., 2018): (1) immature spermatozoa
(presence of cytoplasmic droplets); (2) head abnormalities (double-headed, round-headed, or headless sperm); (3) neck abnormalities (bent
or folded necks); (4) tail abnormalities (folded, curled, or short tails);
and (5) compound abnormalities (multiple defects in the same spermatozoon). The total malformation rate was calculated as follows:
(Number of abnormal sperm / Total sperm counted) × 100%, and the
proportion of each abnormality category was also determined.

*2.6.*
*Placental Efficiency and Placenta Dissection*

The presence of a vaginal plug was used to confirm copulation, and
E0.5 was defined as the midpoint of the dark cycle on the day of plug
detection. Fetuses and placentas were collected at E18.5. Pregnant mice
were euthanized by CO 2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation.
After dissection, placental and fetal wet weights were recorded.
Placental efficiency was calculated for each fetus-placenta pair as the


ratio of fetal weight to placental weight (fetal weight/placental weight)
(Fowden et al., 2009; Hayward et al., 2016), and the litter mean was
used for statistical analysis. Each placenta was processed immediately:
some were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS for histological
analyses, whereas the remaining samples were snap-frozen in liquid
nitrogen for subsequent mRNA and DNA analyses. For histological and
molecular analyses (PAS, IHC, RT-qPCR, Western blot, and ChIP-qPCR),
2–3 placentas were randomly selected from each dam, and at least six
placentas from three independent dams per group were included.

*2.7.* *Periodic acid-schiff and immunohistochemical staining for mouse*
*placental tissue analysis*

Placental tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 [◦] C for 24 h,
dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and
100%), and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin Section (5 µm thick) were
prepared and stained with Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) reagent (AB-PAS,
EK, China). Placental morphology was examined, and images were acquired using a digital slide scanner (KF-PRO-005-EX, KFBIO, China).

For immunohistochemical staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval (citrate buffer,
pH 6.0, 95 [◦] C for 20 min). The sections were blocked with 5% bovine
serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 [◦] C with rabbit anti-CD31 antibody (Ab182981,
Abcam, UK; 1:200 dilution). After washing with PBS, the sections were
incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary
antibody (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAB
chromogenic staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were
scanned and collected, and ImageJ software (NIH, USA) was used to
quantify the cross-sectional areas of the spongiotrophoblast and labyrinth layers, as well as placental microvascular density (5 random fields
per section, 3 sections per placenta).

*2.8.* *Reverse Transcription Quantitative PCR*

Placental tissues stored at − 80 [◦] C were homogenized in Trizol reagent (15596018, Thermo, USA) using an electric tissue grinder
(SpeedMill plus, Jena, Germany). Total RNA was extracted by
chloroform-isopropanol precipitation (Pharmaceutical Reagent, China),
and RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop
spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). RNA was reverse-transcribed into
cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (RR036A, Takara, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Primers for key placental
development-related genes and nutrient transporter genes were
designed based on the reference sequences of the mouse mm10 genome
(sequences listed in Appendix Table 1 and 2) and were commercially
synthesized. RT-qPCR was performed using a StepOnePlus real-time
quantitative PCR system (ABI, USA) with SYBR Green Master Mix
(A6001, Promega, USA). Gapdh was used as the internal reference gene,
and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2 [-][ΔΔ][CT ]

method. Each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates, and at
least three biological replicates per group were included (n ≥ 3).

H3K9me2, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, H3K27ac, and H3 (internal reference). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with
HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature.

**3.** **Results**

*3.1.* *Paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR impairs sperm quality in F0 male mice*

After 20 weeks of exposure to Wi-Fi-derived RF-EMR, male mice in
the exposure group exhibited a marked reduction in cauda epididymal
sperm concentration, decreasing to (3.91 ± 1.59) × 10⁶/mL compared
with (30.73 ± 4.32) × 10⁶/mL in the sham-exposed group ( *p <* 0.001)
(Fig. 1A).

The total sperm abnormality rate in the RF-EMR exposure (RE) group
increased markedly to 89.51%, which was significantly higher than the
29.33% observed in the sham-exposed (SE) group ( *p <* 0.001) (Fig. 1B).
Notably, the frequencies of head abnormalities (43.36% vs. 4.11%,
*p <* 0.001) and compound abnormalities (12.59% vs. 3.07%, *p <* 0.001)
were significantly higher in the RE group (Fig. 1C). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in the frequencies of immature spermatozoa (2.80% vs. 3.64%, *p* = 0.533) or
neck abnormalities (16.08% vs. 15.48%, *p* = 0.724) (Fig. 1C).

**4.** **Discussion**

The rapid advancement and widespread adoption of wireless
communication technologies have substantially increased personal
exposure to radiofrequency electromagnetic fields (RF-EMF). Consequently, public concern has grown regarding the potential adverse
health effects associated with Wi-Fi-derived RF-EMR exposure.
Numerous studies have shown that Wi-Fi-derived RF-EMR may impair
male fertility, as evidenced by abnormal testicular histology, altered sex
hormone levels, and reduced sperm motility. However, the intergenerational health risks associated with paternal exposure remain poorly
understood. According to the International Commission on Non-Ionizing
Radiation Protection (ICNIRP), the whole-body exposure limits,
expressed as the specific absorption rate (SAR), are 0.08 W/kg for the
general population and 0.4 W/kg for occupational exposure
(International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation, 2020). In the
present study, the estimated whole-body SAR ranged from 0.125 to
0.5 W/kg. Importantly, because of allometric differences in metabolism,
the same SAR imposes a greater physiological burden in mice than in
humans. Using this exposure paradigm, we demonstrated that chronic
paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR exposure in mice reduced fetal weight and
impaired placental efficiency in the offspring. Notably, we identified a
sex-specific effect in this murine model, with male offspring exhibiting
more pronounced placental impairments than female offspring,


indicating a male-biased susceptibility to paternal RF-EMR exposure.
Given the greater physiological impact of the applied SAR in mice, these
findings cannot be directly extrapolated to humans at equivalent SAR
levels. Nevertheless, potential intergenerational risks cannot be
excluded, particularly among occupationally exposed populations
whose SAR levels may approach those used in the present study.
Therefore, our findings do not warrant public alarm but support the
consideration of preconception health recommendations for occupationally exposed men and suggest that regulatory agencies re-evaluate
whether current exposure limits, which are primarily based on thermal effects, adequately account for potential non-thermal, epigenetic,
and sex-specific outcomes.

Both clinical and animal studies have demonstrated that exposure to
Wi-Fi-derived RF-EMR reduces sperm viability and motility while
increasing the incidence of morphological abnormalities (Vijay et al.,
2025; Avendano et al., 2012; Maluin et al., 2024). Our findings are
consistent with these reports. In the present study, prolonged RF-EMR
exposure significantly reduced epididymal sperm concentration and
increased the frequency of sperm morphological abnormalities in male
mice, particularly head abnormalities and compound abnormalities.
Additionally, we evaluated the proportion of immature spermatozoa but
found no significant difference between the exposed and control groups.

Several epidemiological and experimental studies have specifically
examined the effects of paternal RF-EMR exposure on offspring health.

exposure to an increased risk of preterm birth, certain congenital

exposure is associated with placental dysfunction. Furthermore, we
demonstrate for the first time that chronic paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR
exposure induces sex-specific alterations in placental labyrinth layer
proportion and disrupts the expression of placental developmental
genes, with male offspring exhibiting greater susceptibility.

sex. To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these
sex-dependent effects, we investigated the influence of paternal
Wi-Fi-derived RF-EMR exposure on the expression of placental nutrient
transporters. Quantitative analysis of 12 key nutrient transporter genes
revealed extensive downregulation in male placentas from the paternal
exposure group, with seven transporters being significantly affected.

glucose transporters *Slc2a1* and *Slc2a3*, the amino acid transporter
*Slc3a2*, and the organic ion and iron transporters *Slc22a3* and *Slc40a1* . In
contrast, female placentas from the paternal exposure group exhibited
significant downregulation of only three transporters: *Slc2a3*, *Slc38a1*,
and *Slc40a1*

male-specific susceptibility to paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR exposure, characterized by widespread downregulation of placental nutrient transporter expression in male offspring, whereas female offspring exhibited
only limited changes. This sexually dimorphic pattern of transporter

placental nutrient transport capacity, providing a mechanistic explanation for the more pronounced impairment in fetal growth observed in

paternal environmental exposure.

Paternal RF-EMR exposure induced sex-specific histone modifications in F1 placentas, raising the critical question of how these epigenetic alterations are transmitted across generations. Sperm carry
multiple layers of epigenetic information, including DNA methylation,
histone retention, and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), all of which have
been proposed as potential mediators of intergenerational inheritance
(Liao et al., 2025). However, following fertilization, the zygote undergoes extensive epigenetic reprogramming, during which most
sperm-derived DNA methylation patterns and chromatin modifications
are globally erased and subsequently re-established, thereby substantially limiting their potential for direct transmission (Xavier et al., 2019;
Burton and Torres-Padilla, 2025). In contrast, sperm-borne
ncRNAs—including small RNAs (miRNAs, piRNAs, and tsRNAs) as

—
well as long non-coding RNAs can largely evade this reprogramming
process and regulate early embryonic gene expression, making them the
most plausible carriers of paternally acquired environmental information (Liao et al., 2025; Tomar et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025).

Consistent with this hypothesis, accumulating evidence indicates
that sperm-borne ncRNAs play a central role in mediating paternal
environmental effects on offspring development. A variety of paternal
exposures have been shown to remodel the sperm ncRNA repertoire,
thereby influencing offspring metabolic homeostasis (Hernandez et al.,
2024; Park et al., 2026; Argaw-Denboba et al., 2024). Furthermore,
sperm-derived ncRNAs have been implicated in early placental lineage
specification and the regulation of placental function (Jodar, 2019; de la
Iglesia et al., 2026), providing a biologically plausible mechanism
linking paternal RF-EMR exposure to the sex-specific placental phenotypes observed in the present study.

Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms by which RF-EMR alters the
sperm ncRNA landscape remain largely unclear. Because RF-EMR is a
well-recognized inducer of oxidative stress (Houston et al., 2016; Shokri
et al., 2020), and oxidative stress has been shown to profoundly reshape
the sperm small non-coding RNA (sncRNA) profile (Ren et al., 2022;
Moazamian et al., 2025), we propose that chronic Wi-Fi exposure may
remodel the sperm ncRNA repertoire in F0 males through oxidative
stress-dependent pathways. Notably, the epididymis is likely the primary site where these alterations occur. In contrast to developing germ
cells within the testis, which are protected by the blood–testis barrier,
spermatozoa traversing the epididymis are directly exposed to luminal
fluids and extracellular vesicles enriched in somatic cell-derived
ncRNAs, thereby undergoing extensive post-testicular RNA remodeling

toxicity or hormonal disruption following RF-EMR exposure, the present
study provides the first mammalian evidence that chronic (20-week)
paternal Wi-Fi exposure impairs fetal growth through a placentamediated mechanism. Moreover, while intergenerational effects have

long-term paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR exposure leads to a male-biased susceptibility in placental efficiency and nutrient transporter expression,

environmental information. Second, the present study assessed fetal and
placental outcomes only at 18.5 dpc, thereby missing dynamic changes
during early and mid-gestation; longitudinal studies covering multiple
developmental windows are therefore needed.

**5.** **Conclusion**

This study demonstrates that chronic paternal exposure to Wi-Fi
–
derived RF-EMR (2.4 GHz, whole-body SAR 0.125 0.5 W/kg, 20
weeks) impairs sperm quality in F0 male mice and adversely affects fetal
growth and placental efficiency in F1 offspring in a sex-dependent
manner, with male progeny exhibiting significantly greater


susceptibility. Specifically, male offspring showed more pronounced
reductions in placental efficiency and labyrinth layer proportion,
accompanied by broader downregulation of nutrient transporters.
Concordantly, paternal RF-EMR exposure induced extensive sex-specific
—elevated
alterations in placental histone modifications in males
H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K9me2/3, and H3K4me2—whereas female
placentas exhibited only decreased H3K4me2. Mechanistically, ChIPqPCR revealed a sex-specific enrichment of repressive histone marks at
transporter promoters: compared with the sham-exposed group, male
placentas from the paternal RF-EMR exposure group displayed increased
H3K27me3 occupancy at *Atp1a1* and *Slc22a3*, and elevated H3K9me3 at
*Atp1a1* and *Slc2a1*, whereas female placentas showed only decreased
H3K27me3 at *Slc3a2*
. Collectively, these findings indicate that male
offspring are more vulnerable to adverse intrauterine programming
following chronic paternal Wi-Fi RF-EMR exposure, and that sex
—
dependent epigenetic dysregulation particularly at the level of
placental histone modifications and nutrient transporter

—
expression likely contributes to the male-biased impairments in fetal
development. This study highlights the importance of incorporating
offspring sex as a key biological variable in reproductive risk assessments of paternal RF-EMR exposure and raises important considerations
for preconception health guidance in occupationally exposed
populations.
