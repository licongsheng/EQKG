1. Introduction


a b s t r a c t

In the present study, we investigated the effects of chronic exposure (14 and 28 days) to a 50 Hz, 0.5 mT
extremely low-frequency magnetic field (ELF-MF) on the NMDAR and AMPAR subunit expressions and
rat spatial learning and memory. Using the Western blotting method, we found ELF-MF exposure
specifically decreased the expressions of GluA2 in the EC post 28 day exposure and GluA3 of AMPAR
subunits in the PFC after 14 day exposure, while it did not affect the AMPAR subunit expression in the
hippocampus at both time points. As for NMDAR subunits, 14 day ELF-MF exposure significantly
increased the levels of GluN2A and GluN2B in the hippocampus. Moreover, the levels of GluN1 and
GluN2A were enhanced in the EC and PFC after two weeks of ELF-MF exposure. Interestingly, 28 day
ELF-MF exposure induced a different expression pattern for NMDAR subunits. The increased GluN2A
expression observed at 14 day post ELF-MF exposure was recovered after prolonged exposure in the
hippocampus and PFC. In the EC, the increased expression of GluN1 achieved to control level and,
specifically, a decrease in GluN2A level was observed. Surprisingly, neither 14 nor 28 day ELF-MF did
affect the rat spatial reference memory as assessed by water maze. These results indicate that
the dynamic and brain-region specific changes in ionotropic glutamate receptor expression induced
by ELF-MF are insufficient to influence the rat spatial learning ability.

The fast excitatory neurotransmission in the brain is predominantly mediated by N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs)
and α -Amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) (Dingledine et al., 1999). These receptors mediate the
basic information processing in the brain and play a pivotal role in
synaptic plasticity including long-term potentiation and long-term
depression (Ho et al., 2011; Shepherd and Huganir, 2007). These
cellular processes mediated by AMPARs and NMDARs are thought to
underlie almost every aspects of brain functions including perception, learning and memory, sleep and wakefulness, attention and
motor control (Ho et al., 2011; Shepherd and Huganir, 2007;
Wollmuth and Sobolevsky, 2004). Moreover, the dysfunction of
NMDARs and AMPARs has been showed to affect the synaptic
efficacy and induce excitotoxicity, which is associated with numerous neurological diseases (Lipton and Rosenberg, 1994).

Functional AMPARs in the brain assemble in heteromeric
tetramer configurations and the vast majority is GluA1/2 and
GluA2/3 heteromers (Isaac et al., 2007). NMDARs also function
as heteromeric assemblies containing multiple GluN1 subunits
in combination with at least one type of GluN2 (Blahos and
Wenthold, 1996). The proper expressions of AMPAR and NMDAR
subunits depend on the neurochemical environment and are
affected by numerous environmental factors. Among these environmental factors, iatrogenic and microwave irradiations recently
have attracted great attention. Acute iatrogenic ionizing radiation
resulted in an early decrease in tyrosine phosphorylation and
removal of NMDARs from the cell membrane (Wu et al., 2012),
while chronic ionizing radiation specifically increased the levels of
GluN1 and GluN2A subunits in the cortex and hippocampus (Liang
et al., 2008; Shi et al., 2006). Moreover, previous studies found that
900-MHz microwave radiation reduced the expressions of GluN1
and GluN2A subunits at mRNA level in the hippocampus and
decreased the amount of NMDARs at the post-synaptic membrane
(Mausset-Bonnefont et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2008).

electric power and by the electrical equipments such as TV sets,

the amplitude of event-related brain potential in a dichotic

visual discrimination task (Crasson et al., 1999). With higher field
strengths (0.6 mT, 50 Hz), ELF-MF exposure caused impairments in
selective attention, working memory and semantic memory
(delayed word recognition) (Preece et al., 1998).

A previous study found that chronic ELF-MF exposure
decreased binding affinity of NMDARs via influencing Ca [2] [þ] signaling (Manikonda et al., 2007). However, so far, little is known about
the potential effects of ELF-MF exposure on subunit expressions of
AMPARs and NMDARs, which are closely related to cognitive
functions. In the present study, we used the Western blotting
method to detect the AMPAR and NMDAR subunit levels in the

spatial learning and memory-related brain structures including
hippocampus, entorhinal cortex (EC) and prefrontal cortex (PFC)
and revealed region-specific effects of ELF-MF exposure on their
expression. These results might provide some useful cues to
explain the ELF-MF exposure-induced alterations in neuronal
functions and spatial cognition.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Subjects

All procedures involving animals were in accordance with the guidelines for
the care and use of laboratory animals in the Third Military Medical University. A
total of 60 adult male Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 220–250 g were used in the
present study. The rats were housed in thermoregulated (22–24 1C) Plexiglas cages
(4–6 rats per cage) with food and water ad libitum and 12 h dark: 12 h light cycle
(light off at 07:00 p.m.). The animals were randomly assigned to four treatment
groups. Group I/II received sham- and ELF-MF-exposure for 14 days, respectively,
while group III/IV were administrated with sham- and ELF-MF-exposure for 28
days. After the exposure, some animals (5 pairs from Group I/II, 6 pairs from group
III/IV) were immediately used for Western blotting to detect the AMPAR and
NMDAR subunit expressions, while others (10 pairs from Group I/II, 9 pairs from
group III/IV) were tested for spatial reference learning and memory in a water
maze.

2.2. ELF-MF exposure system

The exposure system used in the present study was similar to the one used in
previous study (Xiong et al., 2013). In general, The ELF-MF was generated by using a
round coil electromagnet, obtained with a winding of 1000 turned copper wire (Ø
1.0 mm) constructed on a glass fiber cylinder. The electromagnet was supplied with
sinusoidal current (50 Hz), which is generated by a waveform generator (33522 A,
Agilent Technologies, California, USA) with preprogrammed signals. The generator
controls the strength and frequency of the voltage across the solenoid. The
intensity of current in the solenoid is monitored by a current meter, which is
connected in series with the solenoid. The magnetic induction of magnetic field is
measured with a fields meter (PMM 8053B, Narda, Italy). When adjusting the
current strength, sinusoidal magnetic field with average magnetic induction of
0.5 mT can be produced in the center of the coils.
During the exposure period, the centre of each cage was 15 cm vertical below
the poles and exposed with an average 50 Hz, 0.5 mT ELF-MF with a uniformity
of 7 2.0%. The conditions for sham exposure groups were the same to the exposed
groups, except that the exposure system was turned off. The ELF-MF exposure was
performed repeatedly in every afternoon (2:00–6:00 p.m.).


2.3. Protein detection by western blotting

After the exposure, the animals were slightly anesthetized with intraperitoneal
injection of 2.5% sodium pentobarbital (2 ml/kg) and sacrificed by decapitation.
Their brains were rapidly extracted and immersed in cold phosphate buffer saline
(PBS, 0.01 M). The regions of the hippocampus, PFC, and EC cortices were dissected
on ice guided by stereotaxic coordinates. Tissues were then homogenized in icecold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (P8340, Sigma) for 60 min
and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 1C. The supernatant was
harvested, and the concentration of total protein was measured. Samples were
mixed with sample loading buffer and then denatured for 10 min at 95 1C.
Equivalent amounts of protein were loaded onto 5–10% SDS-PAGE mini-gels, and
prestained protein ladder (26617, Thermo) was used as molecular weight markers
on each gel. After electrophoresed and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad),
non-specific binding sites were blocked with 5% non-fat dried milk in TBST (0.05%
Tween-20 in 0.05 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.6, and 150 mM NaCl) for 2 h at room
temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 1C with primary
antibodies. The primary antibodies and dilutions were used: rabbit anti-GluA1,
1:1000 ( AB1504; Millipore); rabbit anti-GluA2, 1:500 (Ab-880, Sigma); rabbit antiGluA3, 1:500 (ab87609; Abcam); rabbit anti-GluN1, 1:1000 (ab17345; Abcam);
rabbit anti-GluN2A, 1:500 (M264, Sigma); mouse anti-GluN2B, 1:500 (ab93610,
Abcam). Membranes were washed three times for 5 min each with TBST and
incubated for 2 h with horseradish peroxidase-linked secondary antibodies. After
washing with TBST three times for 5 min each, the bands were then incubated with
substrate solution (SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Kit; 34077; Thermo)
for 5 min and exposed to X-ray film. Quantitative analyses of Western blots were
performed by determining the intensity of each band on the developed films with
Quantity One software. To confirm equal loading of the samples on the gel, the
membranes were incubated with mouse anti- β -actin (A5316, Sigma) and processed
as described above. The density of each target band was normalized to the density
of β -actin band. Data for the exposure groups were expressed as percentages of the
values measured in control animals.

2.4. Water maze training

The circular water basin (50 cm high, 120 cm diameter) located on an elevated
platform 20 cm above the floor was used in the Morris water maze test.
A transparent Plexiglas platform (9 cm diameter) was constantly placed 2 cm
below the surface of opaque water (25 71 1C) in one of the four quadrants across
all the trials. Clearly visible cues outside the basin were provided for orientation.
The behavior of rat was recorded by a ceiling-mounted automatic tracking system,
which can measure a number of parameters, including latency to find the platform,
swimming distance and swimming speed. During the training sessions, the rat
facing the wall was gently placed into the water from one of the four cardinal
points of the compass. The starting positions varied from trial to trial in a quasirandom order. The rat had to swim until it climbed onto the escape platform. Rats
were guided to the platform by the experimenter if they failed to locate it within
60 s. The rat was allowed to stay on the platform for 10 s and break for 30 s after
each trail. The rats were subjected to a total of eight trials.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean 7 standard error. Differences between groups
were analyzed by unpaired t-test or two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Statistical
significance was set as Po 0.05.

4. Discussion

The present study, to the best of our knowledge, systematically
investigated the effect of ELF-MF exposure on the AMPAR and
NMDAR subunit expressions in the spatial learning and memoryrelated brain regions for the first time. We found that chronic
ELF-MF exposure differently influenced the AMPAR and NMDAR
subunit expressions in distinct brain regions. Surprisingly, these
complex alterations in AMPAR and NMDAR subunit levels are not
causally linked to water maze performance.

Chronic ELF-MF exposure dynamically affected the relative
levels of different subunits of the AMPARs. In the EC, a specific
reduction in the expression level of GluA2 was observed only at 28
days post ELF-MF exposure. However, In the PFC, in relation to the


increased expression of GluA3 subunit after 14 day repeated
treatment, a reversion to control values was observed after the
prolonged exposure. It is noteworthy that this complex phenomenon for AMPAR subunits has been observed in other studies.

In frontal cortex, the increased AMPAR subunit expression at 1 day
after application of methamphetamine achieved to the control
level post 7 day repeated treatment (Simoes et al., 2008). In
addition, in the PFC, repeated stress or antidepressant treatments
increased the AMPAR subunit expression. Consistent with our
findings, these alterations were partially or completely returned
to control values after prolonged treatments (Barbon et al., 2011;
Yuen et al., 2012). So far, the exact mechanism underlying the
time-dependent change in AMPAR subunit level remains unclear.
The restoration of GluA3 expression might reflect a tolerant
response to the long-term ELF-MF exposure. Functional AMPA
receptors in the brain assemble in heteromeric tetramer configurations and the vast majority in the brain is GluA1/2 and GluA2/3
heteromers (Dingledine et al., 1999; Isaac et al., 2007; Lu et al.,
2009). In the EC and PFC, ELF-MF-elicited decrease of the GluA2
and GluA3 level might cause hypofunction of AMPARs and
thus affect the synaptic efficacy and the capacity for plasticity.
Interestingly, in the hippocampus, neither 14 day nor 28 day ELFMF exposure did alter the levels of all AMPAR subunits. Similarly,
previous studies found that neither total nor intracellular AMPAR
subunits in hippocampus were altered by iatrogenic irradiation
(Shi et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2012). These results suggested that the
AMPAR subunits in hippocampus might be less vulnerable to the
environmental radiation.

ELF-MF exposure also changes the relative levels of different
subunits of the NMDARs. In the EC and PFC, expressions of GluN1
and GluN2A were significantly increased. This is in agreement
with earlier reports indicating a specific increase in these subunit
expressions in the hippocampus and cortex post ionizing radiation
or esophageal acid exposure (Banerjee et al., 2011; Liang et al.,
2008; Shi et al., 2006). Unlike in the EC and PFC, the expressions
of GluN2A and GluN2B, but not GluN1, were increased in the
hippocampus. The region-varying effects of ELF-MF may reflect
different regional radiosensitivities for these subunits, which has
been proposed by Todorovic and colleagues elsewhere (Todorovic
et al., 2006). Interestingly, upregulation of GluN2A expression
observed at earlier time point was restored to the control level
after prolonged exposure in the hippocampus and PFC. In the EC,
the increased expression of GluN1 was recovered and, specifically,
a decrease in GluN2A level was observed. The time-dependent
changes for GluN1 and GluN2A have been observed in the frontal
cortex and CA2/3 post repetitive treatment of drugs that could
enhance the glutamatergic transmission (Gori and Girardi, 2013;
Simoes et al., 2008). Given that ELF-MF exposure also could
increase the synaptic excitability (Jaffe et al., 1980), the restoration
and downregulation of NMDAR subunit expression post-long-term
ELF-MF exposure thus might reflect the homeostatic mechanism
to offset ELF-MF-induced enduring overactivity of excitatory
synaptic transmission.

Most functional NMDARs in the brain are GluN1/2A and GluN1/
2B heteromers (Blahos and Wenthold, 1996). GluN2B-NMDARs
have a higher binding affinity to CaMKII (Barria and Malinow,
2005) and allow for more plasticity than do GluN2A-NMDARs
(Akashi et al., 2009; Clayton et al., 2002). 14 day ELF-MF exposure
leads to relatively lower level of the GluN2B subunits compared to
the GluN2A subunits in the EC and PFC. Conversely, 28 day ELF-MF
exposure induced an opposite effect in the EC. Increased GluN2A
expression subunits result in more incorporation of this subunit to
NMDARs (Barria and Malinow, 2002). Thus, ELF-MF exposure
might dynamically affect proportion of GluN1/2B receptors in
relation to GluN1/2A receptors and thus influence the synaptic
plasticity. Previous study using the same exposure parameters

Time- and density-varying ELF-MF exposures have been shown
to induce diverse and even contradictory effects on spatial learning and memory (Cui et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2008). The exposure to
ELF-MF for 1 mT � 3 days, 1 mT � 12 consecutive weeks and
8 mT � 20 min decreased the rat learning proficiency (Cui et al.,
2012; Jadidi et al., 2007; Lai et al., 1998). Conversely, the exposure
to 2 mT ELF-MF for 4 weeks has been showed to cause a beneficial
effect (He et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2008). However, not all studies
have found significant effects of ELF-MF exposure on learning and
memory. Previous studies found that acute (0.5 mT, 50 Hz) or
prenatal (5 mT, 50 Hz) ELF-MF exposure did not affect the rat
spatial learning in radial-arm maze (Sienkiewicz et al., 1996a;
Sienkiewicz et al., 1996b). Consistent with these findings, the
present study found that chronic exposure to 0.5 mT ELF-MF did
not influence the rat performance in the water maze. Currently,
the effects of ELF-MF exposure on cognitive functions are inconsistent without a clear dose-response relationship. These discrepancies in outcome may be largely due to the different
combinations of ELF-MF exposure time and intensity and the
experimental variables like the age and sex of the animals.

However, there are several limitations in the present study.
First, we detected total subunit expression of ionotropic glutamate
receptors, while we did not explore whether ELF-MF exposure
affected AMPARs and NMDARs-mediated post-synaptic currents
and synaptic plasticity. Second, we only assessed the rat spatial
memory with the Morris water maze. It cannot rule out the
possibility that other cognitive functions such as attention and
working memory are not affected by chronic ELF-MF exposure.
Overall, ELF-MF exposure induced complex changes in ionotropic
glutamate receptor subunit expressions in the hippocampus-ECPFC circuitry. Reasonably, we speculate that these alterations
might affect the rat spatial performance. In fact, the present study
found that neither 14 day nor 28 day ELF-MF exposure did affect
the rat spatial reference learning and memory. These results imply
that the complex changes in AMPAR and NMDAR subunit levels
were insufficient to influence the rat spatial learning ability in

water maze.
